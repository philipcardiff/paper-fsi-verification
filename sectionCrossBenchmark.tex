This section draws together the findings of Section~\ref{sec:benchmark_suite} by theme.
In this suite the strength of a verification statement is usually limited by the quality of the reference, the number of refinement levels that could be afforded, or the iterative error, or by sub-nominal convergence whose cause is only partly identified.
Verification also exposed defects that agreement with a reference would not have shown; they are collected in Section~\ref{sec:defects}.
The observations are specific to this suite and to one code family, and are not offered as universal.

Table~\ref{tab:orders} collects the observed orders, from which three observations follow.

\emph{Observed orders can depart from the nominal order.}
Among the category-A cases, the ring added-mass problem exhibits near-second-order temporal convergence, while the amplitude-type quantities of the Womersley tube exhibit near-second-order spatial convergence.
Independently of reference quality, the collapsible channel also shows near-second-order behaviour, in space against its category-B reference (orders 1.6, 2.1 and 2.5 against oomph-lib extrapolated to zero mesh size) and in time by self-convergence (1.8 and 2.0).
In the Womersley tube second order in time requires a flux-consistent treatment of the artificial tube ends; with the usual mixed velocity condition the temporal orders fall towards one, from an $O(\Delta t)$ end-flux inconsistency rather than from the coupling, which the exact reference and fluid-only runs identify (Section~\ref{sec:womersley}).
With the flux-consistent ends the finest solutions lie within $0.12\%$ of the continuum solution ($0.2\%$ for the attenuation, at the linear-theory level).
In the ring added-mass problem the exact reference allows the remaining $-0.13\%$ frequency error at a practical resolution to be attributed to the BDF2 phase error.
The manufactured solution shows that a nominally second-order code need not reach second order in every quantity: the partitioned fluid pressure converges at first order, and the solid displacement loses its order on the finest pair (Section~\ref{sec:mms_fsi}).

\emph{Two levels do not establish convergence, and super-nominal orders are a warning.}
In Turek--Hron FSI3 the displacements agree with the independent reference to $0.3\%$ on the coarsest of three levels, but this is a crossing: with refinement they move away from it, to $0.85\%$ ($u_y$ amplitude) and $2\%$ ($u_x$), while their changes shrink at observed orders of three and above (Section~\ref{sec:fsi23}).
In the 3-D pressure-pulse tube the change in $u_{r,\max}$ grows on the third level, and in FSI1 the third and fourth levels confirmed convergence towards Featflow but showed that $u_x$, first order, crosses the reference between 4x and 8x.
FSI2 still has two levels, so the large-amplitude Turek--Hron case remains the least completely verified.
Orders well above the formal value, such as the FSI3 displacement orders of three to seven, are interpreted as pre-asymptotic; no super-convergence is claimed, and they are not used for extrapolation.

\emph{Coupled limit-cycle QoIs can converge sub-nominally when the components behave well.}
A self-excited amplitude is set by a balance of small differences between large loads, so a sub-nominal order in the in-phase fluid load appears as a sub-nominal order in the amplitude.
In Turek--Hron FSI3 the lift amplitude converges at an observed order of $1.2$, and a set-up with a path-dependent mesh motion and a zero-gradient wall pressure gives an observed displacement order of about one (Appendix~\ref{app:lessons}).
Two statements must therefore be kept apart: ``our result disagrees with the reference'' and ``our result is not yet resolved''.
Against the Featflow table the FSI3 differences ($0.96$--$3.4\%$ except in the frequency and drag mean) lie within the uncertainty of that table, whose levels are non-monotone in these quantities and whose time history drifts by $2.7\%$ in lift amplitude, so they cannot be attributed to either code.
Against the sequence of~\citet{Failer2020}, resolved to $\approx0.3\%$, the $0.85$--$2.1\%$ differences in the displacements are a property of our sequence, which moves away from the reference while its changes shrink and is not asymptotic.
A reference comparison cannot replace a convergence study of one's own, and that study needs more than two levels.

\emph{Combined space--time refinement conceals the balance of errors.}
Where space and time have been separated, the balance differs strongly: in the 3-D pressure-pulse tube the temporal error at the fine-level $\Delta t$ ($0.1$--$0.2\%$) is several times smaller than the level-2-to-3 change ($0.3$--$1.0\%$), in the Mok lid-driven cavity it is an order of magnitude below the spatial error, in the ring added-mass problem it dominates the frequency, and in the Womersley tube it is a tenth or less of the finest-mesh error for the profile, wall phase and attenuation, comparable for the wall amplitude and flow phase, and larger for the wave speed, whose finest-mesh error is only $1.4\times10^{-5}$.
Scaled studies cannot show which dominates unless supplemented, as for the 3-D pressure-pulse tube, by time-step checks on the finer levels; the scaled FSI3 sequence of Section~\ref{sec:fsi23} has none.

The pattern is not simply that displacements converge faster than forces.
Time-mean integrated forces (Turek--Hron drag, vertical force in the flexible-bottom cavity) are often the \emph{fastest}-converging quantities, plausibly because they are dominated by a global pressure balance.
The slowest are of three kinds:
(i) oscillation amplitudes of forces that result from near-cancellation of larger contributions, above all the Turek--Hron lift amplitude, which is also the QoI most sensitive to the coupling tolerance;
(ii) phase- and attenuation-type quantities, or small components, such as the Womersley wall phase and attenuation ($\mathrm{Im}(k)$ is 14\% of $\mathrm{Re}(k)$) and the transverse tip displacement of FSI1; and
(iii) the response amplitudes of a self-excited oscillation, set by a small imbalance of large loads, such as the Turek--Hron FSI3 displacements, whose changes shrink at orders of three and above while they move away from an independent reference by up to $2\%$.
A benchmark should therefore report several QoI types (a displacement, a mean force, an oscillatory force amplitude or phase, and a frequency where applicable), and the slowest QoI on which a claim is made determines the resolution required.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\subsection{Iterative and coupling errors}\label{sec:iterative_errors}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

The iterative error is the least systematically quantified component of the error budget in the present suite.

\emph{Direct tolerance evidence.}
Turek--Hron FSI2 contains a coupling-tolerance sweep, and it shows that a tolerance of $10^{-5}$ inflates the lift amplitude by $13\%$ (1x) and $30\%$ (2x): the effect grows with refinement, so a tolerance chosen on a coarse mesh can contaminate a fine-mesh result.
FSI3 (uniform family) shows the same mechanism in time: at a fixed tolerance of $10^{-5}$ the step-to-step force noise grows as $\Delta t$ falls, inflating extrema-based force amplitudes while leaving the displacements unaffected (Section~\ref{sec:fsi23}).
Two strict-test sweeps (Table~\ref{tab:tol_sweeps}) bound the iterative error directly at fixed discretisation.
In the ring added-mass problem (strong level, mesh 2, 100 steps per period) reducing the tolerance from $10^{-4}$ to $10^{-8}$ changes the wet/dry ratio by at most $1\times10^{-5}\%$ of the exact ratio with IQN-ILS, and by at most $3\times10^{-5}\%$ below $10^{-5}$ with Robin--Neumann ($1\times10^{-3}\%$ at $10^{-4}$), against a change of $0.015\%$ between meshes 2 and 4; the IQN-ILS amplitude loss ($1.15\%$ per period) and the $0.04\%$ frequency difference between the two couplings do not change with the tolerance, so neither is an iterative error.
In the collapsible channel (fluid level 3, $\Delta t = 1/160\,\mathrm{s}$) Robin--Neumann changes the maximum error against the reference by $0.002\%$ of the peak between $10^{-4}$, the baseline tolerance, and $10^{-5}$, against $0.03\%$ between fluid levels 3 and 4; a tolerance of $10^{-6}$ could not be reached (the first step stalled at the iteration limit).
With the strict test, IQN-ILS did not complete this calculation of the collapsible channel at any tolerance from $10^{-3}$ to $10^{-6}$: it stalled at the iteration limit early in the run for $10^{-5}$ and $10^{-6}$, and the coupling diverged after the external-pressure ramp for $10^{-3}$ and $10^{-4}$.
The IQN-ILS results for the collapsible channel in Section~\ref{sec:collapsible}, computed with the legacy test, therefore rest on iterations that were accepted unconverged (Section~\ref{sec:convergence_method}), which is consistent with the IQN-ILS/Robin--Neumann gap that does not decrease with refinement there.
Elsewhere, the iterative error is bounded only indirectly.

\emph{A converged-looking QoI can carry an accumulated coupling error.}
This is the evidence for the recommendation of Section~\ref{sec:convergence_method}.
In a preliminary calculation of the Hessenthaler flap on a different fluid mesh from those of Section~\ref{sec:hessenthaler} (IQN-ILS, tolerance $10^{-4}$, $\Delta t = 0.002\,\mathrm{s}$, 40\,000 steps to $t = 80\,\mathrm{s}$), the legacy test $\min(r_1, r_2)$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:coupling_residuals} accepted steps after about three iterations once the flow had become quasi-steady, since $r_2$, normalised by the total tip deflection of $\approx17\,\mathrm{mm}$, was small (median $3.5\times10^{-5}$) whatever the reduction within the step.
After $t = 1\,\mathrm{s}$ every step was accepted with $r_1 > 10^{-4}$ (median $\approx6.8\times10^{-2}$; $87$--$91\%$ of steps with $r_1 > 10^{-2}$), the fluid interface lagged the solid by $\approx0.03\,\mathrm{mm}$, and the flap tip crept upwards at $+0.003$--$0.005\,\mathrm{mm/s}$ ($16.84$, $16.90$ and $16.98\,\mathrm{mm}$ at $t = 30$, $60$ and $80\,\mathrm{s}$), by more than the spatial differences under study, although neither the fluid nor the solid alone drifted.
Restarted from the $80\,\mathrm{s}$ state with the strict test $\max(r_1, r_2)$ (10--11 iterations per step), the tip settled within $\approx0.3\,\mathrm{s}$ to $17.0195\,\mathrm{mm}$, flat to $\pm0.0002\,\mathrm{mm}$ over $7.5\,\mathrm{s}$; strict restarts from different legacy-test states did not return to one value ($17.02$ and $16.90\,\mathrm{mm}$), so the accumulated error leaves history in the solution, and a clean result requires the strict test from $t = 0$ (\texttt{paper-data/hessenthaler\_phaseI\_verification.md}).
A steadiness check on the QoI, for example a change below $0.04\%$ over the last second, would have accepted the drifting solution.
The lesson is general to partitioned codes: a convergence measure normalised by a large total quantity can be met without converging the step, and the error it admits is invisible to checks on the solution itself; the residual reduction at acceptance should be reported.
Accepted unconverged steps do not always matter: in the Mok lid-driven cavity the legacy test accepted about $98\%$ of the steps with $r_1$ above the tolerance, yet the strict test changes the periodic statistics by at most $0.024\%$, two orders of magnitude below the spatial changes (Section~\ref{sec:mok}). Without the strict-test repeat, however, neither outcome could have been known in advance.

\emph{Iterative floors can limit refinement studies.}
In the collapsible channel the IQN-ILS/Robin--Neumann difference does not decrease with refinement and, at the finest fluid mesh, is comparable with the remaining discretisation error and the reference uncertainty; in the blob in treacle the time study cannot be continued below $\Delta t = 0.03125\,\mathrm{s}$ because the changes reach the tolerance level.
In both, the iterative error rather than the discretisation bounds what can be verified.

\emph{Internal agreement is not external verification.}
IQN-ILS and Robin--Neumann agree to $0.03\%$ (ring added-mass frequency), $2.1\times10^{-4}$ (Womersley tube), $0.01\%$ (FSI1), and $0.07\%$ (3-D pressure-pulse tube QoIs).
This is a consistency check of the implementations against each other, not a bound on the iterative error of either (both can share an incomplete field solve, and their discrete interface conditions differ); it says nothing about the shared discretisation error, nor about whether the common limit is the continuum solution.
FSI3 (uniform family) shows that the two need not agree to the coupling tolerance when the Robin--Neumann scheme modifies the discrete interface condition.

\emph{Coupling algorithms can affect solution quality as well as cost.}
In the ring added-mass problem, IQN-ILS produces a mesh-dependent amplitude loss that Robin--Neumann does not (mechanism unknown); and the cheaper algorithm is case dependent (IQN-ILS in the ring added-mass problem, FSI3 and, narrowly, the Womersley tube; Robin--Neumann in the 3-D pressure-pulse tube).

Two observations follow.
First, the toolchain belongs in the verification matrix.
The aliasing defect produced a reproducible difference between two platforms that looked like a platform or round-off effect, and a study on one platform, at any resolution, would have reported either the correct or the incorrect result without warning; its mechanism lies in OpenFOAM's field algebra and is not specific to solids4foam (Appendix~\ref{app:lessons}).
Second, a defect can switch on with refinement: the interpolation degeneracy affected levels 2 and 3 of the 3-D pressure-pulse tube but not level 1, so a mesh study with it compares different interpolation operators and is not evidence about the discretisation.
Cross-platform replication, decomposition invariance and symmetry checks are inexpensive and were decisive; they should accompany refinement studies of coupled codes.

\subsection{Reliability of published benchmark values}\label{sec:published_reliability}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

Of the ten problems without an exact reference, four have a category-B reference, independent of the code under test and with its own convergence evidence at the resolution used: Turek--Hron FSI1 (Featflow; SU2--CalculiX), FSI3~\citep{Failer2020}, the collapsible channel (oomph-lib) and the Mok lid-driven cavity (Kratos); the flexible-bottom cavity (SU2--CalculiX) would be a fifth \hl{[pending merge of the SU2 data]}.
Only two of these, the Featflow FSI1 table and the sequence of~\citet{Failer2020}, are published values; the other two references were computed for this study.
Of the remaining cases, the independent references of FSI2 and of the 3-D pressure-pulse tube are not converged at the resolution used, that of the beam in cross-flow converges over five levels but is published only to three digits, with a stated accuracy of $1\%$, and for an inflow that had to be corrected (category C), and the Hessenthaler flap and the blob in treacle have second solutions only from the same group or code lineage (Table~\ref{tab:published}).
This is a statement about the specific values used, not about the quality of the FSI benchmark literature in general.

The table illustrates the central distinction of Section~\ref{sec:verification_methodology}: a widely used benchmark problem does not automatically provide a trustworthy benchmark solution.
The same problem can carry several published ``reference values'', and an $18\%$ difference in a quoted force amplitude exceeds the change of every FSI3 quantity between our two finest levels (at most $1.1\%$).
Disagreements between codes can originate in the discretisation of the reference rather than of the code under test (3-D pressure-pulse tube), so a discrepancy with a published curve should not be resolved by tuning the code; and same-lineage references, though useful for regression and for diagnosing set-up differences, cannot expose common-mode errors.

Cost rises by roughly four orders of magnitude from the exact-reference problems to the finest three-dimensional calculations (Table~\ref{tab:cost}), and the completeness of the evidence falls in the opposite direction: the inexpensive two-dimensional and axisymmetric problems have three or more levels in space and time and exact or independent references, whereas the three-dimensional problems have three levels with sub-nominal or undefined orders (3-D pressure-pulse tube) or a published definition that had to be corrected (beam in cross-flow); for the Hessenthaler flap a strict-test run on the third fluid level costs $\approx1.8\times10^{3}$ core-hours, and the fluid convergence of its principal QoI is not established. Even in two dimensions a third level can cost an order of magnitude more than the second (Turek--Hron FSI3, uniform family: 40.8\,h on 32 ranks at 4x, without further strong scaling), and the third FSI3 level of Section~\ref{sec:fsi23}, with $3.9\times10^{5}$ fluid cells, took about eight days on a 128-core node.
This illustrates one practical reason why rigorous convergence evidence becomes scarcer as geometric and computational complexity increase, and it motivates a tiered suite (Section~\ref{sec:recommended_suite}).
Which error source dominates is case dependent: in the thin-walled two-dimensional collapsible channel the solid discretisation can dominate ($17.4\%$ versus $0.44\%$ for linear versus cubic solid), whereas in Turek--Hron FSI1, once the solid is of high order, the remaining error is that of the fluid and of the interface load (Section~\ref{sec:fsi1}).
\emph{An independent code.}
The FEBio ring calculations of Section~\ref{sec:ring} give one cost point for a different method on a problem of the same size.
In a free-slip configuration of the ring comparison (slip interface and walls; Section~\ref{sec:ring} uses no-slip walls), on the mesh-2 discretisation (768 solid and 2\,304 fluid cells or elements), at the strong level with 100 steps per period over four periods on one core, FEBio's monolithic fluid--FSI solver took $2\,033\,\mathrm{s}$, and solids4foam took $224\,\mathrm{s}$ with IQN-ILS and $315\,\mathrm{s}$ with Robin--Neumann coupling.
The processors differ (a shared node with AMD EPYC 9684X processors for FEBio, an exclusive node with AMD EPYC 7452 processors for solids4foam), and so do the methods: FEBio solves a weakly compressible fluid with a full Newton method and a direct factorisation at every step, whereas solids4foam uses a pressure-based incompressible fluid solver with partitioned coupling.
These figures therefore describe the cost of these particular configurations; they are not a ranking of the codes.
