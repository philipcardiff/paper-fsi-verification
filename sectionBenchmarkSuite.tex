The benchmarks are ordered by the category of their reference (Table~\ref{tab:ref_hierarchy}): exact references, category A (Section~\ref{sec:exact_refs}); independently converged references, category B (Section~\ref{sec:independent_refs}); partly quantified references, category C (Section~\ref{sec:partly_quantified_refs}); and unquantified references, category D (Section~\ref{sec:unquantified_refs}).
Table~\ref{tab:suite_overview} summarises the evidence, Figure~\ref{fig:solution_gallery} shows representative solutions of the cases (the manufactured solution is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:mms_fsi_case}), and Figure~\ref{fig:geometry_panel} shows the geometries of the cases that have no figure in their own section.
Every case is described under the same headings: what it tests, problem, reference, numerical model, results, independent numerical comparisons and lessons.
The problem paragraph gives what another code needs to set up the case; the numerical model states only the differences from the procedures of Section~\ref{sec:numerical_methods}, which give the schemes common to all cases.
All calculations are made with solids4foam, using the cell-centred finite volume procedures of Section~\ref{sec:numerical_methods} with partitioned coupling and BDF2 time integration unless stated; the coupling scheme is named where it matters, for example solids4foam with IQN-ILS or with Robin--Neumann coupling, and costs are collected in Table~\ref{tab:cost}.
Unless stated otherwise, the fluid and solid meshes are block-structured meshes generated with the OpenFOAM utility \texttt{blockMesh}, and the levels of a mesh study are obtained by multiplying the block divisions of the coarsest level by the refinement factor, with the block topology and gradings unchanged.
The levels of a mesh study are named L1, L2, \dots\ from the coarsest in every case, with two exceptions: uniform square meshes (the manufactured solution and the Mok cavity) are named by their number of cells per side, $n$, to which their time step is tied, and the uniform block-structured Turek--Hron family keeps the names of its refinement factors, 1x to 8x, to distinguish it from the Gmsh family L1--L3 used for FSI3.
Levels of other codes are given in their authors' numbering, except where those codes use the meshes of this paper.
Appendix~\ref{app:reference_values} collects the recommended reference values of every case, so that another code can be compared with them directly.

\subsection{Exact references (category A)}\label{sec:exact_refs}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

The first two problems are linear, small-amplitude problems whose exact solution is obtained from the continuum equations by a one-dimensional Chebyshev collocation calculation that converges to near round-off (category A, exact), so that the total error of the coupled calculation is measured directly.
The collocation scripts are stored with the cases and share no code with solids4foam, although written by the same authors.
The third is a manufactured solution at finite deformation, exact in closed form (Section~\ref{sec:mms_fsi}).

%%-------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsubsection{Ring added-mass problem}\label{sec:ring}
%%-------------------------------------------------------------------

\paragraph{What it tests.}
Added mass, the principal source of instability and slow convergence in partitioned FSI~\citep{Causin2005}, is present in most benchmarks at a fixed, unquantified strength.
This problem varies the added-mass ratio at fixed solid properties, and its wet/dry frequency ratio separates the added-mass coupling from the solid and temporal errors, which the dry and wet runs share.

\paragraph{Problem.}
An elastic ring vibrates freely in its $n=2$ mode inside a fluid-filled rigid annulus (plane strain, linear geometry, amplitude $\approx5\times10^{-4}$ of the radius, effectively inviscid fluid).
The dry ring is compared with three wet levels, weak, moderate and strong, with $m^* = 0.101$, $1.008$ and $10.08$.
The geometry and material parameters are those of Table~\ref{tab:ring_params} and Fig.~\ref{fig:geometry_panel}(a); the three wet levels differ only in the fluid density.
The added-mass ratio is the ratio of the potential-flow added mass of the $n=2$ mode to the modal mass of a thin inextensional ring,
\begin{equation}
m^* = \frac{n^2}{n^2+1}\,\frac{m_a R}{\rho_S h a},
\qquad
m_a = \frac{\rho_F R}{n}\,\frac{b^{2n}+R^{2n}}{b^{2n}-R^{2n}},
\end{equation}
so that a thin ring vibrates at $\omega_{\mathrm{wet}} = \omega_{\mathrm{dry}}(1+m^*)^{-1/2}$; the exact reference is the full continuum solution, not this thin-ring estimate.

\paragraph{Reference.}
The reference is the exact linear eigenvalue of a plane-strain continuum ring loaded by the exact potential-flow added-mass traction.
It is computed by Chebyshev collocation, and refining the collocation changes the frequencies by $\approx5\times10^{-8}$; hence, this reference is categorised as A (exact).
The dry frequency is $\omega_\mathrm{dry} = 2.555042\,\mathrm{rad/s}$, and the wet frequencies are $2.435615$, $1.804513$ and $0.768654\,\mathrm{rad/s}$ for the weak, moderate and strong levels.
The thin-ring frequency with the confined added mass of Brennen~\citep{Brennen1982} agrees with these to $O((h/a)^2)$.
A linearised viscous solution shifts the wet frequencies by at most $-0.073\%$, which bounds the effect of the small viscosity.

\paragraph{Numerical model.}
A quadrant is modelled, with symmetry planes on the axes.
The solid is the small-strain formulation, with the high-order variant unless stated.
The fluid and solid meshes are refined by factors 1, 2 and 4 (L1--L3) at 100 steps per period, and 25--100 steps per period are used on L2.
IQN-ILS and Robin--Neumann coupling are compared; both use the full interface pressure condition~\eqref{eq:interface_pressure} (Section~\ref{sec:coupling_algorithms}), with its mean removed in this enclosed fluid domain.

\begin{itemize}
	\item \emph{Time.} The temporal orders of the frequency are 1.90, 1.90, 1.90 and 1.89 for the dry ring and the weak, moderate and strong levels, consistent with BDF2, and Richardson extrapolation in time brings every frequency within $+0.018\%$ of the exact value. The residual error of $\approx-0.13\%$ at 100 steps per period is therefore the phase error of the time scheme.
	\item \emph{Space.} With the high-order solid the frequency changes by at most $0.03\%$ between meshes, too little to define an order. The wet/dry ratio, in which the common time error cancels, converges at orders 1.90, 1.84 and 1.90 (weak, moderate, strong) to within $-0.004\%$.
	\item \emph{Solid discretisation.} The standard second-order solid is $20.7\%$ too stiff in bending on the coarsest mesh and $1.6\%$ on the finest (order 1.66), the high-order solid within $0.13\%$ on every mesh; yet the wet/dry ratios of the two solids agree to $0.002\%$ on the finest mesh, and with the standard solid the ratio converges at orders 2.36--2.56.
	\item \emph{Viscous layers.} The Stokes layer, $\delta=\sqrt{2\nu_F/\omega}=2.9\times10^{-4}$ to $5.1\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m}$, is not resolved by the fluid mesh at any refinement factor ($\delta/h=0.009$ to $0.036$). On the meshes used, the computed ratios approach the exact inviscid ratio (within $0.004\%$ on the finest mesh), not the finite-viscosity ratio, which lies up to $0.08\%$ below it.
	\item \emph{Coupling.} IQN-ILS and Robin--Neumann agree to $4\times10^{-4}\%$ in frequency and to $0.001\%$ per period in amplitude loss (L2, 100 steps per period); Robin--Neumann needs 3.7 times as many iterations at the strong level (10.2 against 2.75 per step). Both lose amplitude at the rate of the BDF2 dissipation of the time scheme ($0.04\%$ per period at 100 steps per period, also the loss of the dry ring) to within $0.05\%$ per period at every level and mesh (IQN-ILS, strong level: $-0.001\%$, $0.056\%$ and $0.087\%$ per period on L1--L3). With a zero normal pressure gradient on the interface, IQN-ILS lost up to $2.1\%$ per period on the coarsest mesh at the strong level, halving with each refinement, independently of the coupling tolerance, the time step, the amplitude and the viscosity; this was the $O(h)$ error of the omitted wall-acceleration term, and it also shifted the frequency on L1 by $+0.13\%$ (Appendix~\ref{app:lessons}).
\end{itemize}
The exact frequencies and ratios, with our finest-level values, are collected in Table~\ref{tab:refvals_ring} (Appendix~\ref{app:reference_values}).

\paragraph{Independent numerical comparisons.}
Two calculations with codes that share no component with solids4foam reproduce the exact added-mass response (details in Appendix~\ref{app:ring_independent}).
The finite element code FEBio~v4.13~\citep{Maas2012,Ateshian2018,Shim2019}, with a monolithic Newton solver and a weakly compressible viscous fluid, solves the viscous problem with no-slip on the ring and on the rigid wall, on a fluid mesh graded to resolve the Stokes layer.
Its wet/dry ratios agree with the exact finite-viscosity ratio to better than $0.010\%$ at all three levels, and its fitted amplitude decay rate is $0.97$--$1.00$ times the exact viscous rate; in the moderate level, changing the fluid bulk modulus by an order of magnitude, the regulariser stiffness tenfold or the amplitude by half each changed the ratio by less than $0.01\%$.
A partitioned calculation with the inviscid incompressible fluid of SU2~7.5.1~\citep{Economon2016,Economon2020}, a CalculiX~\citep{Dhondt2004} ring and IQN-ILS coupling through preCICE gives finest-mesh ratio errors of $+0.010\%$, $+0.042\%$ and $+0.052\%$ against the inviscid ratio, converging at observed orders of 1.91--1.92 to Richardson values within $0.02\%$ of the exact ratio.
These comparisons give confidence in the solids4foam solution: three different discretisations, a partitioned finite volume, a monolithic finite element and a partitioned finite volume--finite element one, reproduce the exact ratio to $0.1\%$ or better, and on the finest meshes the SU2--CalculiX ratios differ from those of solids4foam by at most $0.06\%$, about the size of the SU2--CalculiX discretisation error there.
They also give insight that the solids4foam runs cannot.
FEBio resolves the Stokes layer, and so verifies the viscous frequency shift and the viscous decay, which the solids4foam meshes do not resolve.
It also shows a difference between the two discretisations: with nodal no-slip, a uniform fluid mesh of 12 radial cells gives a ratio error of $-6.2\%$, so the finite element calculation needs the layer resolved, whereas the cell-centred finite volume calculation with an unresolved layer approaches the inviscid response on the meshes used.

\paragraph{Lessons.}
The wet/dry ratio verifies the added-mass coupling independently of the accuracy of the solid, and the exact reference attributes the remaining error to a single source.
The amplitude, which is not a QoI of the original problem, exposed an $O(h)$ error of a zero-gradient interface pressure, which changed the frequency and the ratio by only about $0.13\%$ on the coarsest mesh but caused an amplitude loss of $2.1\%$ per period; in an enclosed fluid the condition that removes it must also be made consistent with zero net flux (Appendix~\ref{app:lessons}).
Large-amplitude effects are, by design, not tested.
The solids4foam fluid meshes do not resolve the viscous layers. This does not affect the verification of the frequency and of the ratio, which is against the inviscid reference, which the computed ratios approach on the meshes used; its consequence is that the viscous frequency shift and the viscous decay rate are verified only by the FEBio calculation.

Both partitioned couplings converge without difficulty at every level, including the strong level ($m^* \approx 10$), whereas in the collapsible channel (Section~\ref{sec:collapsible}) IQN-ILS does not converge with the all-measure test, fails for part of a wall-density sweep, and Robin--Neumann completes only for $\rho_S/\rho_F \geq 1$.
A nominal mass ratio is therefore not a sufficient measure of how hard a problem is for partitioned coupling.
In the ring the added mass acts on a single smooth, low-frequency mode of a linear, small-amplitude problem, so the interface operator that the coupling must approximate is fixed and effectively of low rank; a few secant updates of IQN-ILS, or the solid impedance estimate of the Robin condition, capture it.
The collapsible channel combines a thin, very compliant wall, whose bending stiffness is small relative to the fluid mass it moves, with large, flow-induced deformation that depends on the history of the flow and excites many coupled wall modes.
We expect the interface operator there to be both stronger relative to the wall stiffness and changing within and between steps, which quasi-Newton and Robin couplings handle least well; this explanation is consistent with the evidence of both cases but has not been tested by a dedicated study.

\subsubsection{Pulsatile compliant tube}\label{sec:womersley}
%%-------------------------------------------------------------------

\paragraph{What it tests.}
Pressure-wave propagation in a compliant vessel combines added mass, viscous attenuation and wall compliance, with separate amplitude and phase quantities.
Womersley's analysis~\citep{Womersley1955, Womersley1957} has been used to verify FSI codes~\citep{Filonova2020, Hessenthaler2020}, usually in a thin-wall, long-wave form.
Here the reference is instead the exact linear solution for a thick wall, with neither approximation.
This matters: for the wall of this case the thin-wall wave speed is $4.7\%$ higher than the exact one, so a thin-wall reference would have hidden every discretisation error studied here.
The truncated tube also tests the treatment of open ends.

\paragraph{Problem.}
A harmonic wave travels along an axisymmetric elastic tube (Fig.~\ref{fig:geometry_panel}c) of inner radius $R = 1\,\mathrm{m}$, wall thickness $h = 0.1\,\mathrm{m}$ and length $L = 15\,\mathrm{m}$, with a linear-elastic wall ($E_S = 20\,\mathrm{kPa}$, $\nu_S = 0.3$, $\rho_S = 1000\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$) and a fluid of $\rho_F = 1000\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$ and $\mu_F = 5\,\mathrm{Pa\,s}$, forced at $f = 0.02\,\mathrm{Hz}$ with a pressure amplitude of $1\,\mathrm{Pa}$ on the axis at $x = 0$; the Womersley number is $\alpha = R\sqrt{\omega\rho_F/\mu_F} = 5.01$ and $\rho_S h/(\rho_F R) = 0.1$.
The outer surface of the wall is traction free; the tube is not tethered, i.e.\ its wall is not restrained axially as in Womersley's tethered-tube model.
The tube ends carry exact travelling-wave data, so that the wave is not reflected, and the initial fields are exact.
The amplitudes ($\approx5\times10^{-4}R$) are small enough for linear theory to hold to well below $10^{-3}$.

\paragraph{Reference.}
The reference is the exact linear solution for a thick linear-elastic cylinder coupled to the full linearised Navier--Stokes equations.
It is computed by Chebyshev collocation, and solutions with 8 and 24 intervals agree to $\approx10^{-11}$; hence, this reference is categorised as A (exact).
The complex wave number is $k = 0.143978 - 0.020727\mathrm{i}\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}$, which corresponds to a wave speed of $0.872796\,\mathrm{m/s}$.
The solution reduces to Womersley's thin-wall equation (Filonova et al., Eq.~41) as $h/R\to0$.

\paragraph{Numerical model.}
The axisymmetric fluid and solid meshes are one-cell wedges, refined by factors 1, 2 and 4 (L1--L3) at 200 steps per period; 50--400 steps per period are used on L2.
The solid is the small-strain formulation with the standard variant, and the coupling is Robin--Neumann, with IQN-ILS in a comparison.
The tube ends carry the exact pressure and the exact normal velocity gradient, and the pressure-equation flux on them is built from the boundary velocity (flux-consistent ends; see \emph{Time} below).
Each quantity is compared through its first Fourier coefficient at the forcing frequency $\omega$: for a history $f(t_j)$ sampled at the $N$ time steps of periods 5 and 6, $a = (2/N)\sum_j f(t_j)\exp(-\mathrm{i}\omega t_j)$, so that $f \approx \mathrm{Re}[a\exp(\mathrm{i}\omega t)]$, with amplitude $|a|$ and phase $\arg a$.
The coefficients change by less than $10^{-4}$ from one period to the next.
The quantities are the axial velocity profile and the flow rate at the section $x = L/2$, and the radial displacement of the inner wall there; the wave speed and the attenuation $\mathrm{Im}(k)$ are obtained from a least-squares fit of $\log\hat{p}(x)$ along the tube at $r = R/2$, where $\hat{p}$ is the Fourier coefficient of the pressure.

\begin{itemize}
	\item \emph{Space.} The profile, flow rate, wall-displacement amplitude and wave speed converge at second order (2.01--2.05; flow phase 1.92), the wave speed to $1.4\times10^{-5}$ on the finest mesh. The wall-displacement phase converges more slowly (order 1.40). The attenuation error is $\approx-2\times10^{-3}$ on all three meshes and at every time step, so it no longer depends on the discretisation. It is consistent with the level at which linear theory holds ($\approx10^{-3}$ in $k$): the attenuation is the small imaginary part of $k$, only $14\%$ of $\mathrm{Re}(k)$, so an error that is a fraction $\epsilon$ of $|k|$ is a fraction of about $\epsilon/0.14 \approx 7\epsilon$ of $\mathrm{Im}(k)$.
	\item \emph{Time.} Over the steps used, every QoI converges at second order (1.91--2.07 from 100/200/400 steps per period, 1.86--2.14 from 50/100/200), in both the 5--6 and the 11--12 period windows. This requires the flux-consistent end treatment: with the usual mixed velocity condition and fixed pressure, the end flux differs from the boundary-velocity flux by a term proportional to $\Delta t$, and the temporal orders fall towards one (0.75--1.84 on every mesh). The flux-consistent treatment leaves an inconsistency of $O(\Delta t\,h)$, as for a standard fixed-pressure outlet, which does not affect the orders at the steps used here (Appendix~\ref{app:lessons}).
	\item \emph{Start-up.} The initial fields, including the boundary values of the old-time solid displacements, are exact; the first-period transient is then $0.2$--$0.3\%$ from 100 to 400 steps per period and does not reach the analysed periods 5--6 (effect $<3\times10^{-6}$; period-to-period change $\leq7\times10^{-5}$).
	\item \emph{Space--time balance.} At 200 steps per period the remaining time error is a tenth or less of the finest-mesh error for the profile, wall phase and attenuation, comparable for the wall amplitude and flow phase, and larger for the wave speed, whose finest-mesh error is only $1.4\times10^{-5}$.
	\item \emph{Coupling.} IQN-ILS and Robin--Neumann agree to $2.1\times10^{-4}$ or better in every QoI (8.6 against 9.3 coupling iterations per step).
\end{itemize}

None; the reference is exact.

\paragraph{Lessons.}
This viscous wave-propagation case complements the ring added-mass problem (inviscid, standing mode) with separate amplitude and phase QoIs.
The reference must be the exact thick-wall solution: the commonly used thin-wall theory differs from it by more than the discretisation errors, so it can confirm neither convergence nor order.
A truncated-domain benchmark verifies its boundary treatment as much as its interior scheme: with a standard end condition the temporal order is sub-nominal although the coupling is not involved, so a sub-nominal temporal order should be traced, for example with fluid-only runs against the exact solution, before it is attributed to the coupling.
The attenuation can be verified only to $\approx0.2\%$, the level at which linear theory holds at these amplitudes.

\subsubsection{Manufactured solution for transient ALE FSI with a finite-strain solid}\label{sec:mms_fsi}
%%-------------------------------------------------------------------
% Source: paper-data/mms_fsi/ (README.md there gives the provenance of every
% number in this subsection).

The case is distributed with solids4foam \hl{[pending: release]}.

\paragraph{What it tests.}
The ring and the Womersley tube are linear: their interfaces move by $\approx5\times10^{-4}$ of the radius, the fluid mesh barely deforms and the solid is small-strain, so they cannot test the mesh motion, the moving-boundary treatment or a finite-strain solid against an exact solution.
The analytic FSI solutions of \citet{Hessenthaler2020}, a different work from the flap benchmark of Section~\ref{sec:hessenthaler}, are restricted to shear motions, in which the interface does not move normal to itself and the fluid domain does not deform.
We therefore use a manufactured solution~\citep{Roache2002}: a closed-form field for a channel with a compliant, finite-strain wall, which satisfies the fluid and solid momentum equations with added body forces and satisfies the kinematic and dynamic interface conditions exactly.
No interface source is needed, so the interface transfer, the mesh motion and the coupling of the code are tested as they are used in every other case.

Manufactured solutions for ALE FSI with a large-displacement St Venant--Kirchhoff solid were constructed by \citet{Etienne2012} (see also \citet{Tremblay2006}), steady and transient, and applied to a monolithic finite element code.
They satisfy traction continuity without an interface source by making the fluid pressure and viscosity manufactured functions along the interface, whose points move only vertically.
The same construction was used to verify a hybridisable discontinuous Galerkin fluid coupled to a continuous Galerkin solid~\citep{LaSpina2020}, and \citet{Yu2015} extended the approach to spring-mounted rigid bodies in two- and three-dimensional flow.
\citet{Fisch2014} applied manufactured solutions to a partitioned environment with a cell-centred finite volume fluid and a geometrically nonlinear membrane, with a source term in the interface traction balance.
The present solution keeps the fluid viscosity constant, lets the interface slide tangentially as well as move normal to itself, and closes the shear traction through the flow field (Appendix~\ref{app:mms}); to our knowledge it is the first such solution with a finite-strain continuum solid applied to a cell-centred finite volume code.
It is exact; hence, this reference is categorised as A, but it is not a physical problem, and it serves code verification only.

Two variants are used.
In the \emph{full} variant the interface moves normal and tangential to itself and tilts, so the fluid cells next to it become non-orthogonal to it; in the \emph{flat} variant the interface stays flat and moves only vertically, so the fluid mesh stays orthogonal at the interface.
Comparing the two separates the effect of boundary non-orthogonality from the rest of the discretisation.

\paragraph{Problem.}
The fluid occupies $0<x<L$, $0<y<h(x,t)$, and the solid, in its reference configuration, $0<X<L$, $H<Y<H+T$, with $L = 2$, $H = 1$ and $T = 0.5$ in consistent units (Appendix~\ref{app:mms}, Table~\ref{tab:mms_params}).
The fluid ($\rho_F = 1$, $\mu_F = 0.01$) has an inlet at $x = 0$, an outlet at $x = L$ and a no-slip wall at $y = 0$.
The solid follows the compressible neo-Hookean law of Section~\ref{sec:solid_model} (Equation~\eqref{eqn:neo_hookean}) in plane strain ($E_S = 10$, $\nu_S = 0.3$, $\rho_S = 1$).
Its lower face $Y = H$ is the interface, its top face $Y = H + T$ is clamped, and its left and right faces, $X = 0$ and $X = L$, are clamped in the full variant and carry the exact displacement in the flat variant.
The density ratio is one, so added mass is strong.
The motion has period one and starts from rest, with zero fields and an undeformed mesh.
In the full variant the interface rises and falls by up to $0.13H$ as a pulsing, travelling wave, which tilts it by up to $14^\circ$, and slides tangentially with a stretch of up to $10\%$; the solid reaches Green--Lagrange strains of $0.26$, rotations of $0.23\,\mathrm{rad}$ and $0.69 \le J \le 1.09$.
The fluid carries a pulsatile through-flow with a travelling vortex train, with a peak speed of about $2.1$ and a Reynolds number of about 200 based on the channel height.
Figure~\ref{fig:mms_fsi_case} shows the exact solution of the full variant at four times of the simulated interval, $0 < t \le 0.4$, the interface motion over one period, and the interface of the flat variant, which rises and falls uniformly.
The manufactured sources are of the size of the physical terms (fluid $|\bb{b}_F| \approx 10$ against $|\partial\bb{v}_F/\partial t| \approx 9$ and $|\bb{\nabla}p_F| \approx 8$), and the errors are measured at $t = 0.2$ and $0.4$.

\paragraph{Construction.}
The solution is built from the solid to the fluid (Appendix~\ref{app:mms}).
The solid displacement is chosen first, in closed form, and its value on the interface prescribes the interface motion.
The fluid velocity is the curl of a stream function, so it is divergence-free on the deforming channel and equals the interface velocity on the interface.
A stream-function term that changes only the shear rate on the interface, and the interface pressure, are then chosen so that the fluid traction equals the solid traction exactly.
The fluid and solid body forces follow from the residuals of the momentum equations and are given in Appendix~\ref{app:mms}.
The construction and the generated evaluation code were checked symbolically and numerically (Appendix~\ref{app:mms}).
The solution was developed with substantial assistance from an AI coding assistant under the authors' direction, as stated in the declaration of generative AI at the end of the paper.

\paragraph{Numerical model.}
The fluid uses Gauss linear gradients, two outer correctors with four pressure correctors and one non-orthogonal corrector, and linear-solver tolerances of $10^{-12}$; the time-derivative correction of the momentum-interpolated face flux is switched off, and at the outlet, where the velocity gradient and the pressure are prescribed, the face flux is built from the boundary velocity rather than from the velocity of the adjacent cell.
The solid is the total Lagrangian formulation with the standard variant (stabilisation factor $0.1$), with the deformed area of its traction boundary evaluated from a surface gradient over the boundary face; its old-time displacements at $t = -\Delta t$, $-2\Delta t$ and $-3\Delta t$ are set to the exact solution, continued smoothly to $t < 0$, so that the start-up from rest is second order.
Appendix~\ref{app:lessons} gives the reasons for these settings.
The fluid mesh moves by a displacement Laplacian with quadratic inverse-distance diffusivity, which moves the cells next to the tilting interface almost vertically, so in the full variant they become non-orthogonal to the interface by up to $13^\circ$.
Two couplings are compared: Dirichlet--Neumann with Aitken relaxation, with the moving-wall velocity and a flux-consistent pressure gradient on the interface, and Robin--Neumann, whose Robin condition includes the viscous and body-force terms of the normal momentum balance~\eqref{eq:interface_pressure}.
The coupling tolerance is $10^{-6}$ with the either-measure test and at most 200 iterations per step; the two couplings give solid displacements that agree to about $2\%$ on every level, so the iterative error does not determine the orders.
IQN-ILS did not complete the case, because its least-squares update inverted the solid cells next to the interface (Appendix~\ref{app:lessons}).
The boundary data are exact (Table~\ref{tab:mms_bc}), and the body forces are applied explicitly, the fluid force at the current cell centres at the new time level and the solid force at the reference cell centres.
The fluid meshes have $n \times n/2$ and the solid meshes $n \times n/4$ square cells, with $n = 16$, $32$, $64$ and $128$ faces on the interface, and $\Delta t = 0.32/n$ ($0.02$ to $0.0025$), i.e.\ space and time are refined together.
The errors are volume-weighted root-mean-square (L2) norms of the pointwise error at the cell centres (Appendix~\ref{app:mms}); the pressure level is fixed by the outlet, so the pressure error is not corrected by its mean.
The quasi-monolithic coupling of the companion paper~\citep{CardiffBatistic2026QM}, run on the same meshes with the same mesh motion and time steps, is a same-lineage cross-check, not an independent reference.

None; the reference is exact, and the quasi-monolithic coupling above is a same-lineage cross-check.

\paragraph{Lessons.}
The exact finite-deformation solution exposed errors in the outlet flux and interface conditions of the fluid and in the start-up and traction boundary of the solid that neither the linear exact problems nor the physical benchmarks of the suite exposed (Appendix~\ref{app:lessons}); fluid-only and solid-only runs against the exact fields were the quickest way to attribute them.
The pair of variants isolates the effect of boundary non-orthogonality, and a boundary correction that vanishes on the OpenFOAM releases used here was found this way (Appendix~\ref{app:lessons}).
The interface traction is dominated by pressure (viscous-to-pressure ratio $\approx0.03$), so the viscous traction path is tested only weakly; a second parameter set with a larger viscosity would test it more strongly.
The partitioned studies refine space and time together, so their orders combine spatial and temporal errors.
The sources are evaluated at cell centres and compared with cell values, which introduces $O(h^2)$ differences that do not affect the orders.
The case is two-dimensional with a compressible solid; a three-dimensional variant and an incompressible solid, which would need a mixed formulation, are possible extensions.

\subsection{Independently converged references (category B)}\label{sec:independent_refs}

The references of the five problems below are numerical solutions computed with codes that share no component with solids4foam, each with its own convergence evidence: oomph-lib for the collapsible channel, the Featflow tables and the sequence of~\citet{Failer2020} for Turek--Hron FSI1 and FSI3, Kratos for the Mok lid-driven cavity, and SU2--CalculiX and FEniCSx for the flexible-bottom cavity.

\subsubsection{Collapsible channel}\label{sec:collapsible}
%%-------------------------------------------------------------------

\paragraph{What it tests.}
A thin, very light elastic wall in a channel combines strong added mass, bending-dominated large deformation and fluid-mesh motion, where partitioned methods are least robust~\citep{Jensen2003, Heil2004, Heil2008}.

\paragraph{Problem.}
An elastic section of the upper wall ($h/L = 0.005$; St Venant--Kirchhoff continuum) collapses under a ramped external pressure by $\approx21\%$ of the channel height and then performs a damped oscillation (analysed to $t = 1.5\,\mathrm{s}$).
The channel has height $a = 1\,\mathrm{m}$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:geometry_panel}d); the elastic section, $5 < x < 15\,\mathrm{m}$ of the upper wall, has thickness $h = 0.05\,\mathrm{m}$ and length $L = 10\,\mathrm{m}$, is clamped at both ends to rigid sections of length $5\,\mathrm{m}$ upstream and $10\,\mathrm{m}$ downstream, and has $E_S = 455\,\mathrm{MPa}$ and $\nu_S = 0.3$ (plane-strain modulus $500\,\mathrm{MPa}$).
The fluid ($\rho_F = 1\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$, $\nu_F = 0.02\,\mathrm{m^2/s}$) is driven by the inlet pressure that sustains a Poiseuille flow of mean velocity $1\,\mathrm{m/s}$, with $p = 0$ at the outlet and parallel flow at both ends; it starts as that Poiseuille flow, with the wall flat, and the external pressure on the wall rises as $p_\mathrm{ext}(t) = 100\,[1 - \cos(\pi t/0.25\,\mathrm{s})]\,\mathrm{Pa}$ to $200\,\mathrm{Pa}$ at $t = 0.25\,\mathrm{s}$.
The QoI is the vertical displacement of the wall midpoint, $x = 10\,\mathrm{m}$.
The Reynolds number is $Re = 50$, based on the channel height and the mean inflow velocity; time is scaled on the advection time, so the Strouhal number is one and the Womersley parameter $Re\,St$, the product that multiplies the time derivative in the non-dimensional momentum equation, is also $50$.
In the problem of \citet{Jensen2003}, as implemented in the oomph-lib tutorial~\citep{Heil2006}, the wall is a massless Kirchhoff--Love beam of thickness $h/a = 1/100$ with an axial pre-stress of $10^3$ times its effective modulus, and the external pressure is applied impulsively.
Four changes make the problem well posed for a continuum solid and for partitioned coupling.
The pre-stress is removed, as a continuum cannot carry the corresponding pre-strain, so the wall deforms in combined bending and stretching rather than as a tensioned membrane.
The wall is thickened to $h/a = 1/20$, the value of \citet{Heil2008}, with the external pressure scaled accordingly.
The wall is clamped at both ends, as a continuum fixed on its end faces is, and the external pressure is ramped over $0.25\,\mathrm{s}$, which removes the impulsive start that limits the temporal convergence of second-order schemes.
The wall is given the density of the fluid, $\rho_S = \rho_F$, without which the partitioned coupling fails at small time steps and on fine fluid meshes; this regularising density shifts the response by $0.28\%$ of the peak.
The published values are therefore purposely not reproduced; the reference below is computed for the modified problem.

\paragraph{Reference.}
The reference is computed with oomph-lib~\citep{Heil2006}, an independent monolithic Newton finite element code whose driver source is stored with the case.
The reference stored with the case is computed at oomph-lib's tutorial resolution ($100\times16$ fluid and 40 beam elements) and Richardson-extrapolated in time (correction $0.06\%$ of the peak displacement; two fluid element types differ by $<0.001\%$). A spatial study with the oomph-lib meshes refined two, three and four times at $\Delta t = 0.00625\,\mathrm{s}$ shows that oomph-lib converges at first order in space on this problem (observed orders 1.24 and 1.07 in the wall-midpoint history), so the tutorial-resolution error is $0.53\%$ of the peak, about twice the $0.28\%$ difference between its first two levels. The fluid-mesh study below is therefore compared with oomph-lib extrapolated to zero mesh size from its three- and four-fold refined meshes; extrapolations from the two- and three-fold and from the three- and four-fold meshes agree to $0.004\%$.
oomph-lib represents the wall as a geometrically nonlinear Kirchhoff--Love \emph{beam}, whereas the solids4foam finite volume solver used here resolves it as a two-dimensional continuum.
This model-form difference is explicitly identified and of order $h/L$. It is $0.05\%$ of the peak for the static deflection.
For the first trough it is estimated by an open-loop replay: the fluid load recorded at every step of one coupled run is applied as a prescribed load, without feedback from the wall motion to the fluid, to the continuum wall alone and to the beam alone.
The two first troughs then differ by about $0.06\%$ of the reference peak displacement ($0.057\%$ at the wall midpoint with 320 beam elements), and the beam trough still deepens at about first order under refinement, so the difference is a lower estimate, not a bound (Supplementary Material).
It is small relative to the history differences of interest ($0.33\%$ on the finest fluid level, L4, below); hence, this reference is categorised as B (independently converged). It exceeds, however, the L4 first-trough difference ($0.02\%$), so a difference below the reference uncertainty plus this contribution, about $0.06\%$ in the first trough, cannot be attributed to either code.

\paragraph{Numerical model.}
The fluid and solid meshes are generated with \texttt{blockMesh}.
The fluid levels L1--L4 have 3\,200, 12\,800, 28\,800 and 51\,200 cells (refinement ratios 2, 1.5 and 1.33); the wall has $160\times8$ cells with the high-order solid unless stated.
The baseline coupling is Robin--Neumann at $\Delta t = 0.00625\,\mathrm{s}$, with IQN-ILS in a comparison; the Robin condition is the acceleration-only form and the IQN-ILS interface has a zero normal pressure gradient (Section~\ref{sec:coupling_algorithms}). On the mesh of the distributed case, adding the viscous term changes the wall-midpoint history by $0.8\%$ of the peak with Robin--Neumann, and by $0.1\%$ with IQN-ILS and the acceleration condition.

\begin{itemize}
	\item \emph{Solid.} In a static wall test the cubic high-order solid is within $0.25\%$ of oomph-lib on all solid meshes and converges to the $0.05\%$ model-form difference; the linear-reconstruction solid has errors from $-16.7\%$ to $-0.22\%$. In the coupled problem on the baseline mesh the solid contributes $0.44\%$ (cubic) against $17.4\%$ (linear).
	\item \emph{Fluid mesh.} On L1--L4 (fixed $\Delta t = 0.00625\,\mathrm{s}$, compared with oomph-lib at the same $\Delta t$, extrapolated to zero mesh size) the maximum difference falls from $4.81\%$ to $1.59\%$, $0.68\%$ and $0.33\%$ of the peak, at orders 1.6, 2.1 and 2.5 (Fig.~\ref{fig:collapsible_mesh}); the L4 first trough, $-0.21610\,\mathrm{m}$, is within $0.02\%$ of the peak of oomph-lib's, $-0.21615\,\mathrm{m}$. Against oomph-lib at its tutorial resolution the same differences stall at $0.53\%$ and $0.50\%$: that apparent floor is the reference's own spatial error ($0.53\%$), not a property of the solids4foam solution.
	\item \emph{Time.} On L1, successive differences of $4.56\%$, $1.33\%$ and $0.34\%$ give orders 1.8 and 2.0 (oomph-lib itself: 1.8 and 1.9).
	\item \emph{Coupling.} IQN-ILS and Robin--Neumann agree to $0.08$--$0.23\%$ of the peak, but this gap does \emph{not} decrease with mesh refinement; with the all-measure coupling test IQN-ILS does not converge on L3, whereas the Robin--Neumann results change by only $0.002\%$ of the peak between tolerances of $10^{-4}$ and $10^{-5}$ (Section~\ref{sec:iterative_errors}). In a wall-density sweep ($\rho_S/\rho_F = 0$--3) IQN-ILS fails to complete for part of the range, whereas Robin--Neumann completes for all $\rho_S/\rho_F \geq 1$.
\end{itemize}
The troughs and peaks on L4 and in the extrapolated oomph-lib solution are collected in Table~\ref{tab:refvals_collapsible} (Appendix~\ref{app:reference_values}).

\paragraph{Independent numerical comparisons.}
A separate monolithic ALE finite element implementation (FEniCSx; quadratic velocity and displacement, linear pressure, on triangles), written and run by the authors of this paper and sharing no code with solids4foam or oomph-lib, solves the same modified problem with a continuum wall on three meshes and four time steps (Supplementary Material).
On its finest mesh its first trough lies within $0.02\%$ of the peak of the extrapolated oomph-lib value and within $0.001\%$ of L4, and its wall-midpoint history within $0.075\%$ of the peak of the extrapolated oomph-lib history.
Against oomph-lib at its tutorial resolution it shows the same $0.5\%$ floor, consistent with that floor being the reference's own spatial error, and at equal time steps its changes of the first trough between successive steps agree with oomph-lib's to $0.003$--$0.014\%$ of the peak.
It thus confirms, with a second continuum discretisation, that the remaining differences from oomph-lib are of the size of the model-form difference and the reference uncertainty; its open-loop replay gives the model-form estimate quoted under Reference.

\paragraph{Lessons.}
This is the strongest independent-code comparison in the suite.
For a thin bending-dominated wall the solid discretisation can dominate the FSI error.
Once the fluid discretisation error falls to a few tenths of a per cent of the peak, it is of the same size as the spatial error of a reference computed at a published tutorial resolution ($0.53\%$), the difference between the two couplings ($0.08$--$0.23\%$) and the model-form difference ($\approx0.06\%$ in the first trough), so closer agreement requires all of them to be reduced.
The temporal error on the finer fluid meshes is not measured.


%%-------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsubsection{Turek--Hron family (FSI1, FSI2, FSI3)}\label{sec:fsi1}\label{sec:fsi23}
%%-------------------------------------------------------------------

The Turek--Hron problems are the most widely used FSI benchmarks, and the multi-level Featflow results published with them are among the most carefully documented FSI references available.
The question examined here is how far such a published multi-level result, whose own discretisation error is only partly quantified, can bound the error of another code.
The present calculations are first assessed by self-convergence; the comparison with the literature then also examines the published values and the differences between their sources.
FSI1 and FSI3 have category-B references; the FSI2 reference is only partly quantified, hence category C, but FSI2 is kept here with the rest of the family.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[scale=1]{figures/HronTurek3-geometry}
	\caption{Turek--Hron benchmark geometry (shown for FSI3; FSI1 and FSI2 share the geometry). Dimensions in metres; the elastic flag (solid) is shaded, and the cylinder is rigid.}
	\label{fig:hronturek_geom}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{What it tests.}
An elastic flag attached to a rigid cylinder in a channel~\citep{Turek2006} (Fig.~\ref{fig:hronturek_geom}).
FSI1 ($Re = 20$, based on the cylinder diameter and the mean inflow velocity, as for FSI2 and FSI3) is steady, with small but geometrically nonlinear deformation; FSI2 ($Re = 100$, heavier and more flexible flag) is a large-amplitude flapping ($u_y \approx \pm80\,\mathrm{mm}$) that strongly distorts the fluid mesh; FSI3 ($Re = 200$, $\rho_S = \rho_F$) is a self-excited, high-frequency oscillation at low mass ratio ($u_y \approx \pm35\,\mathrm{mm}$).

\paragraph{Problem.}
The geometry is that of Fig.~\ref{fig:hronturek_geom}, and the definition is that of~\citet{Turek2006}: a St Venant--Kirchhoff flag with $\nu_S = 0.4$ and a fluid with $\rho_F = 1000\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$ and $\nu_F = 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m^2/s}$, with a parabolic inflow of mean velocity $\bar{U}$, ramped in as $\bar{U}\,[1 - \cos(\pi t/2\,\mathrm{s})]/2$ for $t < 2\,\mathrm{s}$, $p = 0$ at the outlet and no-slip walls.
FSI1 has $\rho_S = 1000\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$, $\mu_S = 0.5\,\mathrm{MPa}$ and $\bar{U} = 0.2\,\mathrm{m/s}$; FSI2 has $\rho_S = 10^4\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$, $\mu_S = 0.5\,\mathrm{MPa}$ and $\bar{U} = 1\,\mathrm{m/s}$; FSI3 has $\rho_S = 1000\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$, $\mu_S = 2\,\mathrm{MPa}$ and $\bar{U} = 2\,\mathrm{m/s}$.
The QoIs are the displacements $u_x$ and $u_y$ of the flag-tip point A and the drag and lift on the cylinder and flag together, per unit depth.
For FSI2 and FSI3 the QoIs follow the benchmark definitions~\citep{Turek2006}: in each period of $u_y$ the mean of a quantity is (max+min)/2 and its amplitude (max$-$min)/2, both are averaged over the analysis window, and the frequency is that of $u_y$.

\paragraph{Reference.}
For FSI1 the Featflow results are tabulated on six levels (finest $\approx10^6$ elements), with a spread across levels of $0.73\%$ ($u_x$), $0.19\%$ ($u_y$), $0.15\%$ (drag) and $0.26\%$ (lift); the finest values ($u_x = 2.2705\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m}$, $u_y = 8.2088\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m}$, drag $14.2943\,\mathrm{N/m}$, lift $0.76375\,\mathrm{N/m}$) are the reference. They are independent and converged over six levels; hence, this reference is categorised as B.
For FSI2 the reference is the Featflow level-4 table, whose own discretisation error is only partly quantified (see below); hence, this reference is categorised as C.
For FSI3 it is the independent six-level sequence of~\citet{Failer2020} (monolithic $Q_2$ finite elements, up to $1.28$ million unknowns, $\Delta t$ down to $0.001\,\mathrm{s}$), which is independent and has its own convergence evidence; hence, this reference is categorised as B. Its level-5$\to$6 and time-step changes are at most $0.3\%$ for the displacements, the drag mean and the lift amplitude, which we take as its uncertainty, although its displacements change at apparent orders above the formal value. Its means and amplitudes are defined as above but averaged over $t = 9$--$10\,\mathrm{s}$ rather than over the last five periods before $t = 6\,\mathrm{s}$. It agrees with the Featflow level-4 table (15\,872 elements, $\Delta t = 0.00025\,\mathrm{s}$) to $0.6\%$, except for the lift amplitude, which it puts $1.7\%$ higher.

Two sets of Featflow values are published for FSI2 and FSI3.
The frequently quoted summary values are the ``ref.''\ rows of the original proceedings chapter~\citep{Turek2006}, which are its level 4 (15\,872 elements) at the smaller of its two time steps, $\Delta t = 0.0005\,\mathrm{s}$ for FSI3 and $0.001\,\mathrm{s}$ for FSI2.
The detailed tables~\citep{FeatflowFSI,Turek2010} are later computations, tabulated per mesh level and time step down to $\Delta t = 0.00025\,\mathrm{s}$ (FSI3) and $0.0005\,\mathrm{s}$ (FSI2) and published with their time histories; the Featflow pages keep the chapter's values only as superseded ``old values'', and none of their rows reproduces them.
We use the detailed tables, which carry convergence information and can be re-evaluated from the histories.
They are also the more trustworthy set: their time steps are smaller, the chapter's own level-4 FSI3 drag amplitude changes by $13\%$ when its time step is halved ($20.00$ to $22.66\,\mathrm{N/m}$), and the independent sequence of~\citet{Failer2020} agrees with the table ($27.91$ against $27.74\,\mathrm{N/m}$), not with the summary value.
The two sets differ by up to $18\%$ (Table~\ref{tab:hronturek_ref}), so a code compared against the summary values would carry an $18\%$ difference in the FSI3 drag amplitude that belongs to the reference, and a comparison must state which set it uses.
Applied to the published Featflow time histories, our post-processing reproduces Featflow's own tabulated statistics to within $0.6\%$ for FSI3 ($0.8\%$ for the drag amplitude, $0.3\%$ for the frequencies) and $0.3\%$ for FSI2, which validates our implementation of the definitions above.

% smaller time step); Featflow page (FeatflowFSI), where the 2006 values sit
% in HTML comments marked "old values". Audit 2026-11-02: none of the 18
% FSI2/FSI3 rows of the Featflow page reproduces the summary values.

Between Featflow levels 3 and 4 the FSI2 values change by $0.9$--$2.5\%$, and the FSI3 values by $3.8\%$ ($u_x$ mean), $4.0\%$ ($u_x$ amplitude), $4.5\%$ (drag amplitude), $2.6\%$ (lift amplitude) and $1.6\%$ ($u_y$ amplitude); only the drag mean ($0.3\%$) and the frequency change by $\lesssim0.3\%$.
For the FSI3 displacements and drag amplitude the Featflow levels 2, 3 and 4 are not monotone ($u_y$ amplitude $35.73$, $34.43$, $34.99\,\mathrm{mm}$; $u_x$ mean $-3.02$, $-2.77$, $-2.88\,\mathrm{mm}$; drag amplitude $28.80$, $26.50$, $27.74\,\mathrm{N/m}$), and the lift amplitude rises by $2.7\%$ per level without slowing, so these quantities are not shown to be in the asymptotic range and no extrapolated value or error bound can be formed from them.
The published level-4 history is not strictly periodic either: its per-period lift amplitude falls from $157.7$ to $153.1\,\mathrm{N/m}$ over the first two of its seven full periods and then stays within $153.1$--$153.8\,\mathrm{N/m}$, so the tabulated amplitude depends on the averaging window by up to $2.7\%$. The variation is not monotone, and its cause is not known; it may be a transient that decays within about two periods.

The level-4 values are therefore uncertain by $\approx3$--$5\%$ in $u_x$ and the force amplitudes and $\approx2\%$ in the $u_y$ amplitude, a scale of their sensitivity rather than an error bound, whereas the frequency and the drag mean are well determined.

\paragraph{Numerical model.}
FSI1 and FSI2 use a uniform block-structured family (1x, 2x, 4x: 5\,336, 21\,344 and 85\,376 fluid and 630, 2\,520 and 10\,080 solid cells; ratio 2 in the plane).
FSI1 is computed with the high-order (cubic) solid (Section~\ref{sec:solid_solver}) on 1x, 2x and 4x, integrated in pseudo-time to $t = 30\,\mathrm{s}$ ($\Delta t = 0.025$, $0.025$ and $0.0125\,\mathrm{s}$) with IQN-ILS and the either-measure coupling test, and FSI2 on 1x and 2x, with $\Delta t$ halved with the mesh \hl{[pending: FSI2 on the Gmsh family]}.
FSI3 is computed on an unstructured, quadrilateral-dominant fluid family generated with Gmsh~\citep{geuzaine2009gmsh}, with three levels (96\,712, 194\,183 and 387\,219 fluid cells; Table~\ref{tab:hronturek_gmsh}). It replaces the block-structured family, whose neighbour cell-size ratios of $2$--$2.8$ through the wake persist under refinement and spoil the observed orders (Appendix~\ref{app:lessons}). Structured layers surround the cylinder, the flag and the channel walls, and elsewhere the cell size grows linearly with the distance from the cylinder and flag, up to a cap; the size field is scaled by $1/\sqrt{2}$ and the layer counts by $\sqrt{2}$ from one level to the next, except the first cell of the blocks at the two flag-tip corners, which stays at about $0.3\,\mathrm{mm}$ on every level, so the family is not geometrically similar there. The effective refinement ratio, from the cell counts, is $r = (387\,219/96\,712)^{1/4} = 1.415$. The solid is refined with the fluid ($430\times24$, $608\times34$ and $860\times48$ cells, matching the fluid faces along the flag), and the time step is reduced by the same ratio ($\Delta t = 2\times10^{-4}$, $1.414\times10^{-4}$ and $10^{-4}\,\mathrm{s}$, constant from $t = 0$), so the study is scaled. Convection is linear-upwind with an unlimited least-squares gradient; the fluid mesh follows a pseudo-elastic equation for its total displacement from the initial mesh (Section~\ref{sec:fluid_mesh_motion}); the flag carries the acceleration-only interface pressure condition $\mathbf{n}\cdot\nabla p = -\rho_F\,\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{a}$, without the viscous term of Equation~\eqref{eq:interface_pressure}, which changes the L1 QoIs by at most $0.22\%$ (Section~\ref{sec:coupling_algorithms}); each time step of the fluid takes three outer (PIMPLE) correctors, each with three pressure correctors and one non-orthogonal corrector, with the linear solvers converged to absolute tolerances of $10^{-9}$; and the coupling is IQN-ILS with the all-measure test at a tolerance of $10^{-6}$, at most 50 iterations, reusing the secant information of the last five steps. The flag is held rigid to $t = 2\,\mathrm{s}$, and the QoIs are taken over the last five full periods before $t = 6\,\mathrm{s}$.

\emph{FSI3 convergence} (Table~\ref{tab:hronturek_gmsh}, Fig.~\ref{fig:fsi3_histories}).
Every QoI except the drag amplitude changes monotonically, with a smaller change on the second refinement. The displacements grow in magnitude, the $u_y$ amplitude by $0.74\%$ and then $0.25\%$ and the $u_x$ mean by $1.77\%$ and then $0.20\%$, at observed orders of $3.1$ ($u_y$ amplitude) and $6$--$7$ ($u_x$), far above the formal two; the frequency converges at order $3.0$, the drag mean at $1.5$ and the lift amplitude, which falls by $1.70\%$ and then $1.13\%$, at $1.2$. \emph{FSI3 time step and coupling.}
The time step is reduced with the mesh, and on this family there is no separate time-step study, coupling-tolerance sweep, coupling-algorithm comparison or solid-only refinement, so the changes of Table~\ref{tab:hronturek_gmsh} are not divided between space and time or between fluid and solid. The coupling takes $5.1$, $5.4$ and $6.0$ iterations per step on average (at most 24) on L1--L3, no step reaches the iteration limit, and every step is accepted with the step-relative residual $r_1$ below the tolerance (median $6\times10^{-7}$). A $4\,\mathrm{ms}$ moving average lowers the lift amplitude by the same $0.13$--$0.14\%$ on every level, so the step-to-step force noise does not grow as $\Delta t$ falls.

\emph{FSI3 against the references}, compared after the sequence was characterised; no parameter was adjusted towards either reference.
The L3 differences from~\citet{Failer2020} are given in Table~\ref{tab:hronturek_gmsh}. The drag mean agrees to $0.17\%$, and the lift amplitude approaches the reference ($+4.5$, $+2.7$ and $+1.6\%$ on L1--L3) and lies within its provisional GCI. The $u_x$ mean and the $u_y$ amplitude cross the reference between L1 and L2, and the $u_x$ amplitude, $0.3\%$ above it on L1, rises further; the displacements thus move away from it as their changes shrink, and their L3 differences ($0.85$--$2.1\%$) exceed the reference uncertainty ($\approx0.3\%$) and their provisional GCIs several times over. Three levels cannot show whether our sequence, which is not asymptotic, turns at finer levels, as orders this high allow, or converges to a different discrete limit, for which the unrefined cells at the flag-tip corners are one untested candidate; no modelling difference is indicated.
Against the Featflow level-4 table the L3 differences are $0.17$--$3.4\%$, within the $2$--$5\%$ uncertainty of that table, whose lift amplitude lies $1.7\%$ below the converged value of~\citet{Failer2020} ($154.3$, $156.1$ and $156.6\,\mathrm{N/m}$ on its levels 4--6).

\emph{FSI3 coupling sensitivity, on a separate family.}
The coupling-tolerance and coupling-algorithm studies of FSI3 were run before the Gmsh family existed, on the uniform block-structured family above (1x, 2x, 4x; $\Delta t = 0.001$, $0.0005$ and $0.00025\,\mathrm{s}$; IQN-ILS, coupling tolerance $10^{-5}$) with a path-dependent mesh motion and a zero-gradient wall pressure (Appendix~\ref{app:lessons}). Its displacements are superseded, but the iterative error it shows depends mainly on the coupling tolerance and the time step, so it remains informative for the force amplitudes. At a tolerance of $10^{-5}$ the step-to-step force noise grows as $\Delta t$ falls (lift $7.3\,\mathrm{N/m}$ rms at the 4x step against $1.7\,\mathrm{N/m}$ at the 2x step) and inflates the extrema-based force amplitudes on 4x to $+15.0\%$ (lift) and $+18.1\%$ (drag) above Featflow, while leaving the displacements unaffected; a tolerance of $10^{-6}$, as used on the Gmsh family, reduces the noise eightfold. Robin--Neumann and IQN-ILS histories differ ($u_x$ $5.1\%$ and $1.9\%$, lift $3.5\%$ and $2.8\%$ on 1x and 2x); the difference decreases with refinement and is attributed to the different discrete interface condition of the Robin--Neumann scheme, not to iterative error.
The recommended reference values of FSI1 and FSI3, with our finest-level values, are collected in Tables~\ref{tab:refvals_fsi1} and~\ref{tab:refvals_fsi3} (Appendix~\ref{app:reference_values}).

\paragraph{Independent numerical comparisons.}
For FSI1, a partitioned solution in which the fluid, the solid and the coupling are all independent of solids4foam, with the vertex-based finite volume code SU2~\citep{Economon2016,Economon2020} coupled through preCICE~\citep{Chourdakis2022} to CalculiX~\citep{Dhondt2004} on the nodes of the 1x--4x fluid meshes, gives Richardson values of $u_y$ and lift within $0.04\%$ and $0.03\%$ of Featflow; its $u_x$ and drag, which its first-order wall shear affects most, converge too slowly to discriminate between the other solutions (\texttt{paper-data/fsi1\_su2\_calculix/}). A solution that keeps our fluid code but replaces the solid and the coupling with CalculiX and preCICE agrees with the Richardson values of the standard-solid study to $0.02\%$ in every QoI (\texttt{paper-data/fsi1\_precice\_calculix/}).
For FSI3, an OpenFOAM--CalculiX solution through preCICE, on one coarse block-structured level with a zero-gradient wall pressure, agrees with solids4foam on the same mesh to $1.1\%$ in the displacements and $1.0\%$ in the frequency and drag mean, but differs by $6\%$ and $15\%$ in the drag and lift amplitudes, which one level cannot attribute; it is not a comparison for the Gmsh sequence (\texttt{paper-data/fsi3\_precice\_calculix/}).

\paragraph{Lessons.}
The de facto standard requires care in use for verification: the two published Featflow sets differ by up to $18\%$, so the source of a reference value must be stated; the Featflow FSI3 table is itself uncertain by $3$--$5\%$ in $u_x$ and the force amplitudes, which an independent finer sequence~\citep{Failer2020} resolves to about $0.3\%$; and the lift amplitude is sensitive to a coupling tolerance adequate for every other QoI.
FSI1 with a high-order solid converges monotonically to within $0.5\%$ of an independent reference on three levels; on 4x its $u_y$ is closer to the reference than that of the standard solid on 8x.
FSI2 is not yet demonstrably asymptotic \hl{[pending: FSI2 on the Gmsh family]}.
FSI3 has a category-B reference through the sequence of~\citet{Failer2020}. Our three-level sequence approaches it in the lift amplitude and agrees with it in the drag mean to $0.17\%$, but its displacements, which agree with it to $0.3\%$ on the coarsest level, move away from it with refinement to $0.85$--$2\%$ at orders of three and above; the agreement on one level was a crossing, and the remaining differences are a property of our unresolved sequence, not evidence about the reference (Section~\ref{sec:cross_convergence}).

\subsubsection{Mok lid-driven cavity}\label{sec:mok}
%%-------------------------------------------------------------------

\paragraph{What it tests.}
A lid-driven cavity with a flexible membrane bottom~\citep{Wall1999, Mok2001, MokWallRamm2001} is one of the oldest partitioned-coupling benchmarks~\citep{Gerbeau2003, kuttler2008fixed, Kassiotis2011}.
The membrane is tension-dominated and deflects by $\approx0.29\,\mathrm{m}$ in a $1\,\mathrm{m}$ cavity under a periodically forced lid; small openings make the problem well posed for an incompressible fluid.

\paragraph{Problem.}
The fluid fills the square $0 \le x \le 1\,\mathrm{m}$, $0 \le y \le 1\,\mathrm{m}$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:geometry_panel}b), with $\rho_F = 1\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$ and $\nu_F = 0.01\,\mathrm{m^2/s}$.
The lid, $y = 1\,\mathrm{m}$, moves in $x$ with the velocity $\bar{v}(t) = 1 - \cos(2\pi t/5\,\mathrm{s})\,\mathrm{m/s}$.
The top $0.125\,\mathrm{m}$ of each side wall is open: fluid enters through the left opening, $0.875 \le y \le 1\,\mathrm{m}$ at $x = 0$, with the linear profile $v_x = \bar{v}\,(8y/\mathrm{m} - 7)$, and leaves through the right opening, at $x = 1\,\mathrm{m}$, where $p = 0$; the openings stay fixed in space as the membrane moves.
The other walls are no-slip.
The bottom of the cavity is a membrane $0.002\,\mathrm{m}$ thick below $y = 0$, clamped at both ends and traction free underneath, with $E_S = 250\,\mathrm{Pa}$, $\nu_S = 0$ and $\rho_S = 500\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$, modelled as a St Venant--Kirchhoff continuum (plane strain and plane stress coincide at $\nu_S = 0$).
The fluid and membrane start from rest and are computed to $t = 70\,\mathrm{s}$.
The QoI is the vertical displacement of the membrane midpoint, $x = 0.5\,\mathrm{m}$, summarised by its peak, trough and mean over $t = 20$--$70\,\mathrm{s}$, after the start-up transient.
This is the group-B definition below.

\paragraph{Reference.}
The published midpoint histories fall into two mutually inconsistent groups (Fig.~\ref{fig:mok_refs}).
In group A (Mok, Wall, Gerbeau and Vidrascu) the openings are defined through the mesh and their size is not stated, so the problem itself is mesh dependent; peaks range from $0.177$ to $0.215\,\mathrm{m}$.
Group B (Vald\'es~\citep{Valdes2007}, Tiba et al.~\citep{Tiba2026}) specifies inflow over a fixed $0.125\,\mathrm{m}$ opening and $p = 0$ at the outlet; the two peaks differ by $2.7\%$.
The Kratos tutorial example~\citep{KratosLDC}, nominally a group-B set-up, fixes only the horizontal mesh displacement of its side walls, so its inlet and outlet nodes slide up the walls with the mesh and both openings shrink by about $15\%$ at the peak; it therefore does not solve the group-B problem, and its peak lies $9$--$11\%$ below the group-B solutions at every level.
\citet{Kassiotis2011} lies between the groups.
None of the published sources provides a convincing spatial and temporal convergence demonstration, and pilot runs show that traction-free openings reproduce neither group (not shown).

The reference taken here is an independent solution of the group-B definition above, computed by a co-author with Kratos Multiphysics~10.3~\citep{Dadvand2010}, with the opening nodes fixed (\texttt{paper-data/kratos\_mok\_lid\_driven\_cavity/}).
Kratos shares no code with solids4foam. Its particulars are a P1/P1 fluid with variational multiscale stabilisation on structured triangle meshes, Bossak time integration, a Q1 total Lagrangian solid with two elements through the thickness, mesh motion by structural similarity, and Dirichlet--Neumann coupling with MVQN at a tolerance of $10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m}$, met in every step of every run.
Its own error sources were studied separately: the time error (order 2.0--2.3) and the coupling tolerance, the solid resolution through the thickness and the solver tolerances (each below $10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m}$) are negligible, and the fluid mesh dominates.
On a fluid-mesh sequence $16^2$--$128^2$ at $\Delta t = 0.0125\,\mathrm{s}$ the trough converges at order 1.9 and the mean at 1.35, but the peak, monotone only from $32^2$, at order 0.4; the peak is therefore quoted as the $128^2$ value with a band down to the extrapolated values, $0.280$--$0.2835\,\mathrm{m}$, and the trough and mean as their extrapolations from the combined sequence ($0.2051$ and $0.2430\,\mathrm{m}$, GCI $0.45\%$ and $0.61\%$).
The one model-form difference is the outlet: solids4foam imposes zero-gradient velocity with $p = 0$, Kratos the natural condition with $p = 0$; imposing $u_y = 0$ at the Kratos outlet changes the peak, trough and mean by $0.5\%$, $1.7\%$ and $1.0\%$, so the two codes can be compared to about $1\%$.
The Kratos solution is independent and has its own convergence evidence, and the one model-form difference is quantified; hence, this reference is categorised as B (independently converged), with a resolution of about $1\%$.

\paragraph{Numerical model.}
The fluid meshes are uniform $n\times n$ meshes, $n = 16$, $32$, $64$ and $96$, refined together with the time step ($\Delta t = 0.2$, $0.1$, $0.05$ and $0.025\,\mathrm{s}$); the membrane has $n$ cells along its length and 8 through its thickness, with the high-order solid.
The coupling is IQN-ILS with a tolerance of $10^{-5}$ and the either-measure test (Section~\ref{sec:coupling_algorithms}).
A $128^2$ run at $\Delta t = 0.025\,\mathrm{s}$, outside the refinement sequence, relaxes the non-final PIMPLE outer correctors ($p$ 0.3, $\mathbf{U}$ 0.7, the final corrector unrelaxed) with residual control, since at $\nu\,\Delta t/h^2 \approx 4$ (against $2.3$ on $96^2$) the unrelaxed outer loop diverges within a coupling iteration (Appendix~\ref{app:lessons}). Kratos solves the velocity and pressure of its stabilised fluid together, without a segregated outer loop, so this change has no counterpart there.

\begin{itemize}
	\item \emph{Self-convergence.} Successive changes in the solids4foam response (the larger of the peak and trough changes, relative to the finest peak) are $1.56\%$, $1.26\%$ and $0.78\%$, dominated from $32^2$ on by the trough; the peak alone changes by $1.53\%$, $0.66\%$ and $0.31\%$; the observed order of the peak is $\approx1.2$ (non-uniform final ratio) and that of the trough below 0.5, so neither sequence is shown to be asymptotic and no limit is extrapolated; the trough is uncertain by at least $\approx1$--$2\%$. A separate time-step study on $64^2$ changes the response by $0.17\%$ and $0.04\%$ of the peak, so the error is dominated by the mesh. The slow convergence of the peak is a property of the case: Kratos shows it too.
	\item \emph{Comparison with the reference.} On the same sequence the peak differs by $-2.0\%$, $-0.25\%$, $-0.70\%$ and $-0.77\%$, the trough by $+5.7\%$, $+4.6\%$, $+2.0\%$ and $+0.71\%$ and the mean by $+1.3\%$, $+1.9\%$, $+0.48\%$ and $-0.13\%$ (Kratos relative to solids4foam), and on the separate $128^2$ runs by $-0.46\%$, $-0.05\%$ and $-0.29\%$ (peak, trough, mean), so the trough and mean differences shrink with refinement while the peak difference stays below $1\%$ from $32^2$ on; the largest difference between the $96^2$ histories is $2.4\,\mathrm{mm}$, $0.8\%$ of the peak. On $96^2$ the solids4foam trough and mean lie within $0.4\%$ and $0.6\%$ of the Kratos reference values, and its peak $0.9$--$2.2\%$ above the Kratos band. The level-by-level differences on $96^2$ are within the $\approx1\%$ resolution set by the outlet condition; the larger gap to the Kratos peak band reflects the remaining discretisation error of the solids4foam peak, which is still decreasing ($0.31\%$ in the last step) and is not extrapolated.
	\item \emph{Comparison with the published values.} Both converged solutions lie above the published group-B peaks (Kratos by $1.1$--$2.4\%$) and troughs (by $4.3\%$); only the mean lies within the published range. The published group-B values are single-mesh results and appear to be under-resolved.
	\item \emph{Solid and coupling.} The standard and high-order solids agree to $0.04\%$: for a tension-dominated membrane the high-order solid gives no benefit, unlike the bending-dominated collapsible channel. IQN-ILS and Aitken give the same response (difference not quantified).
	\item \emph{Coupling test.} With the either-measure test, about $98\%$ of the steps were accepted on one residual measure only, with $r_1$ above the tolerance of $10^{-5}$ (median $3\times10^{-4}$; Section~\ref{sec:convergence_method}). Repeated with the all-measure test on $32^2$ and $64^2$, the peak, trough and mean change by at most $0.025\%$, two orders of magnitude below the changes between meshes, and reusing IQN-ILS modes from earlier steps changes them by at most $0.025\%$; the accepted iterative error is therefore negligible for these QoIs, and the reported values stand. On the other meshes ($16^2$, $96^2$, $128^2$, and $64^2$ at $\Delta t = 0.1\,\mathrm{s}$) the all-measure test stalls later in the run, with IQN-ILS stagnating above the tolerance; the cause is the unrelaxed PIMPLE outer loop of the fluid, which fails to converge in some steps and returns an inexact fluid response that the interface quasi-Newton update cannot converge (Appendix~\ref{app:lessons}). With the non-final outer correctors relaxed, as on $128^2$, every one of these runs meets the all-measure test in every step to $t = 70\,\mathrm{s}$, with at most 5 ($96^2$) to 23 ($128^2$) coupling iterations per step, and the peak, trough and mean change by at most $0.18\%$ ($16^2$) and $0.12\%$ ($96^2$), about a tenth of the corresponding changes between meshes. Rerunning $16^2$--$96^2$ and the relaxed $128^2$ run with a later build, in which the boundary non-orthogonal correction of the moving-wall velocity (Appendix~\ref{app:lessons}) is restored, changes the reported QoIs by at most $0.041\%$ and every change between meshes by less than $0.05$ percentage points.
\end{itemize}

The reference values and our $96^2$ values are collected in Table~\ref{tab:refvals_mok} (Appendix~\ref{app:reference_values}).

\paragraph{Lessons.}
A long-established benchmark can be ill defined, and a published reference implementation can solve a different problem from the one it is cited for: the Kratos tutorial example moves the openings that define group B.
Once the openings are fixed, two independent codes agree to within about $1\%$ and converge towards each other, and both place the converged peak and trough above every published group-B value.

\paragraph{What it tests.}
Steady channel flow over a cavity with a flexible floor~\citep{Tukovic2018}, derived from a fluid benchmark~\citep{Juretic2010}, is the only steady internal-flow problem in the suite, with a large floor deformation ($u_y \approx -0.24\,\mathrm{m}$) that strongly skews the fluid cells next to the clamped ends of the plate.

\paragraph{Problem.}
A channel of height $1\,\mathrm{m}$ passes over a cavity whose floor is an elastic plate, $4\,\mathrm{m}$ long and $0.4\,\mathrm{m}$ thick, clamped at both ends and traction free underneath (Fig.~\ref{fig:cavity_geom_v}).
The inflow is a parabolic profile with a peak of $1.5\,\mathrm{m/s}$ (mean $1\,\mathrm{m/s}$) across the inlet, the outlet has $p = 0$ and the other walls are no-slip; the fluid has $\rho_F = 1\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$ and $\nu_F = 0.01\,\mathrm{m^2/s}$, so $Re = 100$, based on the channel height and the mean inlet velocity.
The plate is St Venant--Kirchhoff ($E_S = 500\,\mathrm{Pa}$, $\nu_S = 0.4$, $\rho_S = 1000\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$) in plane strain.
The QoIs are the steady vertical displacement $u_y$ of point A, at mid-span on the top (wetted) surface of the plate, and the steady vertical force $F_y$ on the plate, per metre depth.
The through-thickness position of the monitoring point changes $u_y$ measurably: the mid-thickness value differs from the top-surface value by $0.5\%$, and the value at the nearest node on the plate underside is $1.2\%$ smaller in magnitude on the finest mesh, so point A is specified here as the point on the top surface, as in~\citet{Tukovic2018}.

\paragraph{Reference.}
The published values of~\citet{Tukovic2018} come from an earlier version of the same code family and are read from a figure (category D).
The reference taken here is instead formed by two solutions computed for this study with codes that share no component with solids4foam, each with a convergence sequence of its own (Table~\ref{tab:cavity_refs}).
The first is a partitioned solution with the vertex-based finite volume solver of SU2~\citep{Economon2016,Economon2020} for the fluid, CalculiX~\citep{Dhondt2004} for the plate (C3D8I hexahedra with geometric nonlinearity, which is exactly St Venant--Kirchhoff) and preCICE with IQN-ILS for the coupling, computed blind by a co-author, before any reference value or solids4foam result was consulted (\texttt{paper-data/cavity\_su2\_calculix/}).
It uses the fluid mesh of the case refined by factors 1, 2, 4, 8 and 16 ($\Delta x = 0.1$ to $0.00625\,\mathrm{m}$; 1\,688 to 432\,128 fluid cells), with the plate refined with it, and reaches the steady state by coupled steps whose size changes the steady values by less than $2\times10^{-5}$ relative.
Its $F_y$ converges at an observed order of about three ($2.94$, $3.08$ and $3.07$ over the three successive triples) to $-5.257784\,\mathrm{N/m}$ (GCI $0.001\%$), and its $u_y$, which is not monotone on the three coarsest levels, at $1.14$ on the three finest, to $-0.240559\,\mathrm{m}$ (GCI $0.046\%$).
The second is a monolithic ALE finite element solution, written and run by co-authors in FEniCSx (quadratic velocity and displacement, linear pressure, on triangles; Supplementary Material), on four levels with the spacings of the solids4foam meshes; its limits are $-0.240544\,\mathrm{m}$ (order $1.28$) and $-5.257801\,\mathrm{N/m}$ (order $2.18$).
The two independent limits agree to $0.006\%$ in $u_y$ and $0.0003\%$ in $F_y$; hence, this reference is categorised as B (independently converged), with uncertainties of about $0.05\%$ in $u_y$, from the GCIs of both sequences, and $0.001\%$ in $F_y$, the GCI of the SU2--CalculiX sequence.

\paragraph{Numerical model.}
Four uniformly refined levels, L1--L4 ($\Delta x = 0.1$, $0.05$, $0.025$ and $0.0125\,\mathrm{m}$), are computed with solids4foam (partitioned, Aitken relaxation, all-measure coupling test on every level), with the pseudo-time step halved with the mesh spacing ($\Delta t = 0.5$ to $0.0625\,\mathrm{s}$), at most 20 coupling iterations per step (40 on L4) and fluid linear-solver tolerances of $10^{-6}$ ($10^{-8}$ on L4).
The face flux in the continuity equation is interpolated with skew correction, since the fluid cells near the clamped ends of the plate are strongly skewed by the deformation.

\paragraph{Results.}
The steady top-surface $u_y$ at point A is $-0.2048$, $-0.2313$, $-0.2382$ and $-0.2400\,\mathrm{m}$ on L1--L4, converging monotonically at observed orders $1.93$ and $1.98$; the extrapolated value ($p = 2$) is $-0.2406\,\mathrm{m}$ (GCI $0.31\%$). The interface force is $F_y = -5.1607$, $-5.2333$, $-5.2512$ and $-5.2561\,\mathrm{N/m}$, with observed orders $2.03$ and $1.86$ and an extrapolated value of $-5.2579\,\mathrm{N/m}$ from the three finest levels (GCI $0.045\%$).
The steady state depends weakly on the pseudo-time step through the pressure--velocity coupling, and this dependence vanishes rapidly under refinement: reducing $\Delta t$ to $0.0625\,\mathrm{s}$ changes $F_y$ by $0.18\%$ on L1, $0.03\%$ on L2 and $0.001\%$ on L3. With $\Delta t = 0.0625\,\mathrm{s}$ on the three finest levels, the extrapolated values are $-0.2406\,\mathrm{m}$ and $-5.2578\,\mathrm{N/m}$, within $0.003\%$ of those above.
Our extrapolated limits, $-0.240583\,\mathrm{m}$ and $-5.257947\,\mathrm{N/m}$, agree with the SU2--CalculiX limits to $0.010\%$ ($u_y$) and $0.003\%$ ($F_y$), and with the FEniCSx limits to $0.016\%$ and $0.003\%$, well within our GCIs ($0.31\%$ and $0.045\%$).
The finest-level and extrapolated values are collected in Table~\ref{tab:refvals_other} (Appendix~\ref{app:reference_values}).

\paragraph{Independent numerical comparisons.}
Besides the two solutions that form the reference, the quasi-monolithic solution of the companion paper~\citep{CardiffBatistic2026QM}, which shares the code and the meshes and is therefore a same-lineage check, agrees with ours to $0.001\%$ in $u_y$ and $0.004\%$ in $F_y$.
A COMSOL solution computed by a co-author (two-dimensional finite elements), on the four mesh spacings above and a fifth at $\Delta x = 0.00625\,\mathrm{m}$, gives $u_y = -0.23621$, $-0.24064$, $-0.24306$, $-0.24429$ and $-0.24492\,\mathrm{m}$ and $F_y = -5.108$, $-5.227$, $-5.295$, $-5.331$ and $-5.349\,\mathrm{N/m}$ \hl{[pending: COMSOL sequence in paper-data; how its $F_y$ is integrated]}.
Both sequences converge monotonically at about first order (observed orders $0.87$--$0.98$ for $u_y$ and $0.81$--$1.00$ for $F_y$), towards about $-0.2456\,\mathrm{m}$ and $-5.37\,\mathrm{N/m}$, about $2\%$ larger in magnitude than the four solutions above.
The offset is not the element order (quadratic plate elements throughout; with quadratic instead of linear fluid elements $u_y$ still tends to about $-0.2445\,\mathrm{m}$) nor the solver tolerance; that sequence used a uniform inflow of the same mean instead of the parabolic one, which may account for it \hl{[pending: COMSOL sequence with the parabolic inflow]}.

The published values of~\citet{Tukovic2018} (read from the vector paths of its figure; per $0.05\,\mathrm{m}$ depth, as confirmed by its first author) and an ANSYS solution computed by a co-author (a three-dimensional slab with symmetry planes) agree with our extrapolated $F_y$ to $0.02\%$ ($-5.257\,\mathrm{N/m}$) and $0.3\%$, but their displacements are $4.3\%$ and $5.4\%$ larger in magnitude.
ANSYS used a linear elastic material with geometric nonlinearity, which there is, to our knowledge, a logarithmic-strain (Hencky-type) law; a plate-only calculation under the same load gives $1.057$ times the St Venant--Kirchhoff displacement with a Hencky law, against $1.054$ for ANSYS ($-0.2536\,\mathrm{m}$, extrapolated at an observed order of $0.9$).
A neo-Hookean law was not available in the code of~\citet{Tukovic2018} at the time (Ž.~Tuković, private communication), so its plate was St Venant--Kirchhoff or linear elastic, and its $4.3\%$ larger displacement is not explained here \hl{[pending: linear-elastic check]}.
Agreement in the integrated interface force therefore does not establish that a reference solved the same structural problem.

\paragraph{Lessons.}
A small GCI from one code does not establish that its limit is correct: with a plain linear interpolation of the continuity flux on the deformed mesh, four levels give a falsely tight force limit, $0.05\%$ from the independent solutions, which only those solutions expose (Appendix~\ref{app:lessons}).
The integrated force is insensitive to the constitutive law of the plate, whereas the displacement distinguishes laws that differ by a few per cent at these strains, so a comparison should report both.

\subsubsection{Beam in cross-flow}\label{sec:beam}
%%-------------------------------------------------------------------

\paragraph{What it tests.}
A thick elastic plate clamped to the floor of a three-dimensional duct~\citep{Richter2012} is the only steady three-dimensional external-flow case in the suite. It tests the coupling of a three-dimensional continuum solid with a three-dimensional flow at small deformation, and it shows how an error in a published problem definition can be identified.

\paragraph{Problem.}
The duct is $(0, 1.5)\times(0, 0.4)\times(-0.4, 0.4)\,\mathrm{m}$, and the plate, $(0.4, 0.5)\times(0, 0.2)\times(-0.2, 0.2)\,\mathrm{m}$, is clamped to its floor; the plate is St Venant--Kirchhoff ($E_S = 1.4\,\mathrm{MPa}$, $\nu_S = 0.4$) and the fluid has $\rho_F = 1000\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$ and $\nu_F = 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m^2/s}$.
The inflow is the bi-parabolic profile of~\citet{Richter2012} with a peak of $0.2\,\mathrm{m/s}$ (see Reference), the outlet is traction free and the other walls are no-slip.
The QoIs are the steady streamwise displacement $u_x(A)$ of the point $A = (0.45, 0.15, -0.15)\,\mathrm{m}$ and the steady drag $F_x$ on the plate.

\paragraph{Reference.}
The published reference values, $u_x(A) = 5.95\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m}$ and $F_x = 1.33\,\mathrm{N}$ with a stated accuracy of $1\%$, are extrapolated from the finest three of five uniformly refined levels of an independent monolithic finite element code, whose displacements converge cleanly~\citep{Richter2012}.
The printed inflow of~\citet{Richter2012}, with a peak of $0.3\,\mathrm{m/s}$, is inconsistent with the stated mean velocity and Reynolds number, and gives $1.8$ times the reference values; the author has confirmed that the reference values were computed with a $0.2\,\mathrm{m/s}$ peak (T.~Richter, private communication), which is the inflow solved here (Appendix~\ref{app:beam}).
The values are published only to three digits, and their sequence is not tabulated; hence, this reference is categorised as C (partly quantified).

\paragraph{Numerical model.}
The half domain $z \le 0$ is computed with a symmetry plane, on four uniformly refined levels, L1--L4 (fluid $8\,112$ to $4\,153\,344$ cells; 8 to 64 solid cells through the thickness), by partitioned transient-to-steady continuation at a fixed $\Delta t = 0.00625\,\mathrm{s}$ with IQN-ILS and the all-measure coupling test at a tolerance of $10^{-5}$.
The interface pressure has a zero normal gradient, which at the steady state omits only the viscous term of Equation~\eqref{eq:interface_pressure}; on the mesh of the distributed case, including it changes the tip displacement by $+1.7\%$, and on that mesh refined twice by $+0.35\%$ (Section~\ref{sec:coupling_algorithms}).
The conformal interface is transferred by direct face mapping.

\paragraph{Results.}
$u_x(A)$ and $F_x$ converge monotonically, at observed orders rising from $1.3$--$1.4$ (L1--L3) to $1.6$ (L2--L4), so the asymptotic range is suggested but not demonstrated, and no error bound is given (Table~\ref{tab:beam_seq}, Appendix~\ref{app:beam}).
L4 gives $u_x(A) = 5.944\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m}$ and $F_x = 1.3272\,\mathrm{N}$, within $0.1\%$ and $0.2\%$ of the reference values, and the conditional Richardson values from L2--L4 lie $0.5$--$0.8\%$ above them, within their stated $1\%$; the comparison was made after the sequence was characterised.
Coupling tolerances from $10^{-4}$ to $10^{-8}$ change both QoIs by at most $2\times10^{-6}$ (relative), and halving $\Delta t$ changes $u_x(A)$ by about $1/240$ of the L3-to-L4 change.
The values are collected in Table~\ref{tab:refvals_other} (Appendix~\ref{app:reference_values}).

\paragraph{Independent numerical comparisons.}
A co-author computed both inflows with Kratos Multiphysics~10.3~\citep{Dadvand2010}, on tetrahedra obtained by splitting the hexahedra of L1--L3, with a total Lagrangian St Venant--Kirchhoff plate and Dirichlet--Neumann coupling with MVQN (Appendix~\ref{app:beam}).
With the corrected inflow its L3 $u_x(A)$, $5.970\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m}$, is $0.44\%$ above our L4 and $0.34\%$ above the reference value, the two codes approaching the displacement from opposite sides, and its extrapolated force agrees with ours to $0.01\%$ ($1.3324$ against $1.3325\,\mathrm{N}$).
With the printed inflow its displacement is $1.759$ times larger (ours: $1.758$), so an independent code also reaches the reference values only with the $0.2\,\mathrm{m/s}$ peak.
Its sequence, with apparent orders of $3.5$ and $1.8$, is not shown to be asymptotic, and the category is unchanged.

\paragraph{Lessons.}
A published definition can contain an error that a convergence study compared with the reference values exposes: the printed inflow gives $1.8$ times the reference values on every level, and the three statements of the inflow in the sources are mutually inconsistent.
Agreement within the stated $1\%$ is reached on the finest level, but a fifth level would be needed to show that the observed order has stabilised \hl{[pending: L5 or author decision]}.

\subsubsection{Three-dimensional pressure-pulse tube}\label{sec:3dtube}
%%-------------------------------------------------------------------

\begin{figure}[htbp]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[scale=1]{figures/3dTube-geometry}
	\caption{Three-dimensional pressure-pulse tube: geometry and monitoring point A.}
	\label{fig:3dtube_geom}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{What it tests.}
A $3\,\mathrm{ms}$ pressure pulse travelling through a fluid-filled elastic tube (Fig.~\ref{fig:3dtube_geom}) is the standard three-dimensional test of partitioned coupling under strong added mass ($\rho_S/\rho_F = 1.2$)~\citep{Formaggia2001, Gerbeau2003, Fernandez2005, kuttler2008fixed, Degroote2009}; most publications report only iteration counts and snapshots.
It also tests non-conformal interface transfer: the interface could be meshed conformally, but a non-conformal (AMI) transfer is used deliberately, to exercise it in a transient three-dimensional problem.

\paragraph{Problem.}
The tube has length $0.05\,\mathrm{m}$, inner radius $0.005\,\mathrm{m}$ and wall thickness $0.001\,\mathrm{m}$, with clamped ends; the fluid has $\rho_F = 1000\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$ and $\nu_F = 3\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m^2/s}$, and the wall $\rho_S = 1200\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$, $E_S = 0.3\,\mathrm{MPa}$ and $\nu_S = 0.3$.
The inlet pressure is $1333.2\,\mathrm{Pa}$ for $3\,\mathrm{ms}$ and is then ramped to zero by $3.1\,\mathrm{ms}$; the outlet pressure is zero, and the response is computed to $20\,\mathrm{ms}$, including the outlet reflection.
The wall is modelled as a three-dimensional Hookean (small-strain) continuum. Published calculations differ in the wall law: the three references below use a St Venant--Kirchhoff continuum, and the Hookean variant is the one studied here, by solids4foam and by the independent Kratos solution below.
At the peak hoop strain of $\approx3\%$ the two laws differ in stress by a relative amount of the order of the strain, which could change the peak displacement by more than the changes between our finest meshes; it is not quantified here \hl{[pending: St Venant--Kirchhoff wall run]}.
The QoIs are taken at point A, on the inner wall at mid-length: the first maximum of the radial wall displacement $u_{r,\max}$, the first minimum of the axial wall displacement $u_{z,\min}$ (before $6.5\,\mathrm{ms}$), the arrival time $t_\mathrm{arr}$, the first time at which $u_r(A)$ reaches half its first peak, and two wave speeds, each the least-squares slope of an arrival time against position at $z = 1$--$4\,\mathrm{cm}$: the wall-front speed $c_\mathrm{wall}$, from the arrival of half the first peak of $u_r$ on the inner wall, and the pressure-front speed $c_p$, from the arrival of half the inlet pressure on the axis.
The wall-front speed is the primary wave-speed QoI; $c_p$ is reported with a time-step uncertainty of about $1\%$ (see \emph{Time} below).

\paragraph{Reference.}
No tabulated values exist; the references are point-A histories read from figures.
\citet{Tukovic2018} used a partitioned finite volume method from the same code lineage, BDF2 at $\Delta t = 2.5\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{s}$ and a single mesh, and its curve is extracted from the vector graphics of the PDF.
\citet{Lozovskiy2019} (monolithic finite elements) and \citet{Eken2016} (monolithic, with a side-centred finite volume fluid and a finite element solid) are independent codes, both with first-order backward Euler (BDF1) time integration at $\Delta t = 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{s}$ and neither with a time-step study; their curves are raster-digitised to $\approx\pm3\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m}$.
All three model the wall as a three-dimensional St Venant--Kirchhoff continuum, not as a shell; the published definition~\citep{Fernandez2005} prescribes the inlet pressure for $3\,\mathrm{ms}$, and the references state no ramp at its switch-off, whereas here it is ramped over $0.1\,\mathrm{ms}$; their meshes differ from ours and from one another.
The references are digitised, two are unconverged in time and one is from the same code lineage; hence, this reference is categorised as D (unquantified).

\paragraph{Numerical model.}
A quarter of the tube is computed with symmetry planes, with Robin--Neumann coupling and BDF2 in fluid and solid; the Robin condition is the acceleration-only form, and adding the viscous term changes the displacement history by $0.017\%$ of its peak (Section~\ref{sec:coupling_algorithms}).
Three geometrically similar block-structured levels, L1--L3 (16\,000, 128\,000 and 1\,024\,000 fluid cells and 6\,400, 51\,200 and 409\,600 solid cells; ratio 2), are computed with $\Delta t$ halved with the mesh ($2.5\times10^{-5}$, $1.25\times10^{-5}$ and $6.25\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{s}$).
The fluid mesh is an O-grid with a square core, and the solid mesh has 4 cells through the wall thickness on L1.

\paragraph{Results.}
All coupling criteria were met at every step (mean 4.8, 3.9 and 3.2 coupling iterations per step on L1--L3). Table~\ref{tab:3dtube} gives the point-A QoIs, and Figure~\ref{fig:3dtube_histories} shows the point-A histories with the published curves.

\begin{itemize}
	\item \emph{Space.} The axial trough $u_{z,\min}$ and the arrival time $t_\mathrm{arr}$ converge monotonically, at observed orders of about 0.87 and 0.74, far below the nominal two; no Richardson estimate is made. The peak radial displacement $u_{r,\max}$ decreases monotonically, but its changes grow ($0.21\%$, then $0.59\%$), so no order can be given; the last change ($0.59\%$) is a scale of the remaining sensitivity, not a bound on the L3 error, and spatial convergence of $u_{r,\max}$ is not established. The wall-front speed $c_\mathrm{wall}$ rises by $1.41\%$ and then falls by $1.40\%$, so it is not monotone and no convergence statement is made on it. The pressure-front speed $c_p$ changes by $0.27\%$, then $0.56\%$, both below its time-step sensitivity on L3.
	\item \emph{Time.} Halving $\Delta t$ on L2 (to $6.25\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{s}$) changes $u_{r,\max}$ by $-0.09\%$, $u_{z,\min}$ by $+0.03\%$, $t_\mathrm{arr}$ by $+0.01\%$ and $c_p$ by $-0.07\%$; halving it on L3 (to $3.125\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{s}$) changes the wall QoIs by at most $0.03\%$ and $c_\mathrm{wall}$ by $0.004\%$. These are at most $15\%$ of the L2-to-L3 changes, so for the wall QoIs the scaled sequence is dominated by the mesh. The halved-step runs were made on a different platform from those of Table~\ref{tab:3dtube}; L2 at the tabulated $\Delta t$, rerun there as a control, reproduces the tabulated values to $10^{-5}$ relative in the wall QoIs ($0.03\%$ in $c_p$). The pressure-front speed is the exception: on L3 halving $\Delta t$ lowers $c_p$ by $0.86\%$, more than its L2-to-L3 change ($0.56\%$). On the axis the pressure rises slowly, over several milliseconds, and carries oscillations of about $0.2\,\mathrm{ms}$ period and $2\%$ of the inlet pressure whose phase and amplitude depend on $\Delta t$; on a slow rise they shift the crossing of the half-inlet level by up to $0.1\,\mathrm{ms}$. We therefore regard $c_p$ as uncertain to about $1\%$, make no convergence statement on it, and use $c_\mathrm{wall}$ as the wave-speed QoI.
	\item \emph{Coupling.} Robin--Neumann and IQN-ILS agree to $0.07\%$ in the QoIs and $0.26\%$ of the peak in the histories.
	\item \emph{Literature discrepancy.} The published $u_{r,\max}$ of the BDF1 references ($\approx0.119$ and $0.124\,\mathrm{mm}$) lie $20$--$25\%$ below the BDF2 values of \citet{Tukovic2018} ($0.157\,\mathrm{mm}$) and of the present calculations (L3 is $0.9\%$ above \citet{Tukovic2018}). Run with the time discretisation of those references (BDF1, $\Delta t = 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{s}$), solids4foam lowers its peak by $0.036$--$0.038\,\mathrm{mm}$, i.e.\ $92$--$115\%$ of the published gap ($86$--$126\%$ within the reading accuracy), and lands within $+2.5\%/-1.4\%$ (L1) and $+1.3\%/-2.6\%$ (L2) of \citet{Lozovskiy2019} and \citet{Eken2016}. BDF1 itself converges slowly in time (still $4.4\%$ low at $\Delta t = 1.25\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{s}$ on L1), so neither published peak is converged in time. Much of the discrepancy is therefore reproduced by the difference in time integration. \citet{Eken2016} uses a finite volume fluid, so the split is between time integrators, not between finite element and finite volume methods; the remaining differences in the wall law, the switch-off and the meshes are not quantified, and the earlier peak time of \citet{Eken2016} ($0.3\,\mathrm{ms}$) is not explained.
\end{itemize}
The point-A QoIs on L3 are also collected in Table~\ref{tab:refvals_3dtube} (Appendix~\ref{app:reference_values}).

The published BDF1 curves in Fig.~\ref{fig:3dtube_histories} are not the raw pixel traces.
A raster curve, read column by column by colour masking, is a line several pixels thick whose centre is quantised to whole pixels, which makes the trace step by one pixel, about the stated reading accuracy of $\pm3\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m}$, from one column to the next; the trace was therefore averaged over five pixel columns and resampled to the published time step, $0.1\,\mathrm{ms}$ (\texttt{3dTube/verification/reference/} of the solids4foam tutorial).
The raw traces were not kept, so the smoothing error cannot be measured on them directly; it can be bounded instead by applying the same smoothing to our L3 history.
Moving averages over $0.2$ and $0.3\,\mathrm{ms}$ lower the L3 radial peak by $0.4$ and $0.9\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m}$ ($0.25\%$ and $0.57\%$) and raise the axial trough by $0.3$ and $0.6\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m}$, a tenth to a third of the reading accuracy; five pixel columns span less than $0.3\,\mathrm{ms}$ wherever the $20\,\mathrm{ms}$ axis of a figure spans more than about 340 pixels \hl{[check: pixel width of the two published figures]}, so the smoothing error is smaller than the digitisation error.

\paragraph{Independent numerical comparisons.}
A co-author computed the problem with Kratos Multiphysics~10.3~\citep{Dadvand2010}, which shares no code with solids4foam: a P1/P1 stabilised fluid on tetrahedra obtained by splitting the hexahedra of the same block layout, a small-strain hexahedral finite element wall, and Dirichlet--Neumann coupling with the multi-vector quasi-Newton (MVQN) method, on L1 and L2 (\texttt{paper-data/kratos\_3dtube/}).
With BDF1 at $\Delta t = 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{s}$ its L1 peak, $u_{r,\max} = 0.12334\,\mathrm{mm}$, lies between the published values of \citet{Lozovskiy2019} and \citet{Eken2016}, and with BDF2 and $\Delta t$ halved with the mesh it is $30.0\%$ higher, against $30.5\%$ in solids4foam, so an independent code confirms that the published discrepancy is mainly one of time integration.
With BDF2 (Kratos on L1 and L2: $u_{r,\max} = 0.16035$ and $0.16096\,\mathrm{mm}$, $u_{z,\min} = -0.08198$ and $-0.08555\,\mathrm{mm}$, $t_\mathrm{arr} = 5.954$ and $5.925\,\mathrm{ms}$, $c_p = 4.578$ and $4.583\,\mathrm{m/s}$), the two codes approach $u_{z,\min}$ from opposite sides (Kratos on L2 is within $0.2\%$ of our L3), but $u_{r,\max}$ rises with refinement in Kratos and falls in ours, so the difference grows from $+0.5\%$ on L1 to $+1.1\%$ on L2 (Table~\ref{tab:3dtube}); $t_\mathrm{arr}$ is $1.4\%$ later at matched levels, from a wall front that is slower in Kratos, a difference not yet attributed to the wall or the fluid discretisation.
With two levels, a different fluid stabilisation and mesh, and the first step of L2 accepted above the coupling tolerance, this solution is independent but not converged, and the reference category is unchanged.

Report the OpenFOAM temporary-reuse/\texttt{\_\_restrict\_\_} hazard upstream and cite the report. Evidence: paper-data/3dtube\_level3\_findings.md, Section 12.6.}

\paragraph{Lessons.}
The case is more useful as an explanation of a literature discrepancy, and as a defect detector, than as a convergence demonstration.
Much of the published code-to-code discrepancy in the peak is reproduced by the difference in time integration, in solids4foam and in an independent code, whose own two-level solution is not yet converged in the peak either.
The axial displacement and arrival time converge at sub-nominal observed orders, and the peak radial displacement and the wall-front speed are not yet in a converging regime.
A wave speed defined by the crossing of a fixed level on a slowly rising signal is sensitive to the time step through the oscillations on that signal, whereas one defined on the wall front is not; the definition of a QoI should be checked for such sensitivity before its convergence is assessed.
Replicating the case on several platforms exposed two defects in solids4foam that no single-platform refinement study would have shown (Appendix~\ref{app:lessons}).

\subsubsection{Hessenthaler flap (Phase I)}\label{sec:hessenthaler}
%%-------------------------------------------------------------------

\begin{figure}[htbp]
	\centering
	% Source: paper-data/hessenthaler_geometry_figure/fig.py (see its README.md).
	\includegraphics{figures/hessenthaler-geometry}
	\caption{Hessenthaler flap, Phase I: (a) the flow domain on the mid-plane $x = 0$ (dimensions in mm; inlet centres at $y = \pm27.15\,\mathrm{mm}$), with the flap clamped where the two inlet pipes join the outlet pipe; gravity acts in $-y$. (b) The L2 fluid mesh ($3.1\times10^{5}$ cells) on a plane $0.3\,\mathrm{mm}$ from the mid-plane, with the undeformed flap and the deformed centreline measured under the Phase I flow~\citep{Hessenthaler2017a}. The refinement boxes above the flap cover the region swept by the flap and the path of its tip.}
	\label{fig:hessenthaler_geom}

\paragraph{What it tests.}
The benchmark of Hessenthaler et al.~\citep{Hessenthaler2017a,Hessenthaler2017b}, a silicone flap in a flow phantom with phase-contrast magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) measurements, is the only problem of the suite with a complex three-dimensional geometry; it has also been simulated with an immersed-boundary method~\citep{Kolahdouz2023}.
It tests large deformation under buoyancy and flow, a non-conformal interface on unstructured meshes, and the iterative error at a steady state.
It is used twice, and the two uses are kept apart: as a mesh-sensitivity study of one fixed mathematical model of the Phase-I steady state, and, separately, as a validation case.

\paragraph{Problem.}
A silicone flap of $11 \times 2 \times 65\,\mathrm{mm}$ is clamped to the wall where two inlet pipes (diameter $21.9\,\mathrm{mm}$, centres at $y = \pm27.15\,\mathrm{mm}$) join an outlet pipe of diameter $76.2\,\mathrm{mm}$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:hessenthaler_geom}); the flow domain is the published surface~\citep{Hessenthaler2017a}, with the outlet pipe extended by $250\,\mathrm{mm}$.
The fluid is an aqueous glycerol solution ($\rho_F = 1163.3\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$, $\mu_F = 12.50\,\mathrm{mPa\,s}$), entering both inlets with parabolic profiles of peak $630$ (upper) and $615\,\mathrm{mm/s}$ (lower), $Re = 1284$, based on the inlet-pipe diameter and the peak velocity of the upper inlet.
The flap ($\rho_S = 1058.3\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$) is about $9\%$ lighter than the fluid; buoyancy alone lifts its tip by $29.50\,\mathrm{mm}$, and the steady flow pushes it back to a measured $16.41\,\mathrm{mm}$.
As in~\citep{Hessenthaler2017b}, the fluid is solved without gravity, so that its pressure excludes the hydrostatic part, and buoyancy is applied to the solid as the net body force $(\rho_S - \rho_F)\,\mathbf{g}/\rho_S$. The inflow and the body force are ramped in together over the first $0.5\,\mathrm{s}$ with $3s^2 - 2s^3$, $s = t/0.5\,\mathrm{s}$.
The outlet has a fixed pressure and the backflow-stabilised traction $\boldsymbol{\sigma}\mathbf{n} = \beta \rho_F \min(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{n}, 0)\,\mathbf{u}$ with $\beta = 0.2$, the value of the benchmark definition~\citep{Hessenthaler2017b,EsmailyMoghadam2011}.
The flap is neo-Hookean ($\nu_S = 0.45$) with the shear modulus $\mu^* = 64.2\,\mathrm{kPa}$, taken from the continuum limit of a zero-flow calibration against the measured buoyant deflection on four nested solid meshes (second-order convergence to $63.95\,\mathrm{kPa}$, GCI $0.42\,\mathrm{kPa}$, $0.7\%$, which contains $64.2\,\mathrm{kPa}$). $\mu^*$ is not recalibrated per mesh, which would change the problem under refinement.
The quantity of interest is the steady vertical tip deflection $u_y$, averaged over $t = 40$--$45\,\mathrm{s}$, when the flow is statistically steady.

\paragraph{Reference.}
No independent numerical solution exists. The quasi-monolithic solutions of the companion paper~\citep{CardiffBatistic2026QM} on the same meshes share the fluid discretisation, the meshes and the code lineage, and are a consistency check, not a reference; hence, this reference is categorised as D (unquantified), and the verification evidence is self-convergence.

\paragraph{Numerical model.}
The solid uses the total Lagrangian displacement formulation on $12 \times 8 \times 65$ hexahedra (6\,240 cells), the same for every fluid mesh, with a Newton--Krylov solve in each coupling iteration.
The fluid takes up to 20 PIMPLE outer correctors per coupling iteration at $\Delta t = 0.002\,\mathrm{s}$, with unlimited least-squares gradients and convection by an unlimited blend of $75\%$ linear and $25\%$ linear-upwind interpolation; unlimited linear-upwind interpolation becomes unstable at the flap-tip corners, and first-order upwind lowers the tip deflection by $17\%$ (Appendix~\ref{app:lessons}).
The fluid mesh moves with a velocity Laplacian, and interface data are transferred with AMI weights.
The coupling is IQN-ILS with the all-measure test at $10^{-4}$ and at most 60 iterations per step; every run starts from rest and is continued to $t = 45\,\mathrm{s}$.
The primary solutions prescribe the acceleration-only form of the interface pressure condition (Section~\ref{sec:coupling_algorithms}), $\mathbf{n}\cdot\nabla p = -\rho_F\,\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{a}$, with $\mathbf{a}$ the wall acceleration, without the viscous term of Equation~\eqref{eq:interface_pressure}, whose effect on this case was not computed; a zero normal gradient is used as a sensitivity check.
The fluid meshes L1--L3 are non-nested octree meshes generated with cfMesh~\citep{Juretic2015cfMesh} from the published surface, with the flap edges captured as feature edges and refinement around the flap and over the path of its tip (Table~\ref{tab:hessenthaler_meshes}, Fig.~\ref{fig:hessenthaler_geom}b); each level scales all target sizes by $\sqrt{2}$, giving effective linear refinement ratios of $1.37$ and $1.39$.

With the acceleration condition the tip deflection increases by $1.8\%$ from L1 to L2 and by $1.0\%$ from L2 to L3, to $16.726\,\mathrm{mm}$; with the zero gradient by $2.2\%$ and $0.4\%$, to $16.724\,\mathrm{mm}$ (Table~\ref{tab:hessenthaler_results}).
The effect of the interface pressure condition, $0.3$--$0.7\%$ on L1 and L2, vanishes on L3.
On L1 and L2 the tip wanders slowly by $0.1$--$0.2\,\mathrm{mm}$ peak to peak over $t = 25$--$45\,\mathrm{s}$, so their window means are uncertain by up to about $0.1\,\mathrm{mm}$; only the L3 solutions settle, to within $0.0002\,\mathrm{mm}$ over the window.
The ratio of the two changes corresponds to an apparent order of about $1.9$ with the acceleration condition and $5.6$ with the zero gradient, and the second change is comparable with the uncertainty of the L2 window means, so the sequences are not shown to be in the asymptotic range, and no observed order, Richardson value or GCI is given.
What can be claimed is a three-level mesh sensitivity: the L3 tip deflection is $16.73\,\mathrm{mm}$ with either interface pressure condition, its last change under refinement is $1.0\%$ or less, and the same-lineage quasi-monolithic solutions change in the same way and lie within $0.09\,\mathrm{mm}$ ($0.5\%$) of it on L3.
What cannot be claimed is an error bound on the L3 value.
On L3, many steps reach the iteration limit with the residual measure $r_1$ stalled at $\approx2\times10^{-4}$ although the step is converged ($r_2 \approx 4\times10^{-12}$); this is a property of a purely relative measure at a steady state (Appendix~\ref{app:lessons}), not an unconverged step.

The remaining tip discrepancy on L3, $2\%$ of the measured value, is about twice the last mesh change, and no experimental uncertainty is available, so it can be neither attributed to the model nor judged.

\paragraph{Lessons.}
For a complex three-dimensional geometry, three affordable non-nested meshes give a mesh sensitivity, not an error estimate, and a validation statement made on one mesh would credit the model with an accuracy that belongs to the discretisation error.
A steady state exposes the limits of a coupling test: a purely relative residual measure cannot be met once the step is converged to round-off (Appendix~\ref{app:lessons}).

\paragraph{What it tests.}
An elastic half-cylinder in a highly viscous channel flow (Fig.~\ref{fig:blob_geom}), proposed by~\citet{Liu2014} to measure the temporal order of a coupled scheme, is strongly damped and undergoes large deformation; it tests the temporal order and the interface force balance of a finite-strain solid at a density ratio of one.

\paragraph{Problem.}
An elastic half-cylinder of radius $0.5\,\mathrm{m}$, centred at $(1.5, -0.5)\,\mathrm{m}$, is fixed at its base to the floor of the channel $[0, 6.5]\times[-0.5, 1]\,\mathrm{m}$.
The inlet velocity is $u_x = g(t)\,(1 + 2y)(1 - y)\,\mathrm{m/s}$, with $y$ in metres and $g(t) = [1 - \cos(\pi t/2\,\mathrm{s})]/2$ for $t \le 2\,\mathrm{s}$ and $g = 1$ afterwards; the outlet is traction free and the walls no-slip.
The fluid has $\rho_F = 1\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$ and $\nu_F = 1\,\mathrm{m^2/s}$, and the solid $\rho_S = 1\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$.
The published definition uses a small-strain solid, which integrates the fluid traction over its undeformed surface and here receives about $12\%$ less $x$-force than the fluid exerts; we therefore use a total Lagrangian neo-Hookean solid with the Lam\'e constants of~\citet{Liu2014}, $\lambda = 500\,\mathrm{Pa}$ and $\mu = 50\,\mathrm{Pa}$, with which the interface forces balance to about $10^{-6}$. A St Venant--Kirchhoff solid is not used, as it softens in compression and collapses at $t \approx 1.8\,\mathrm{s}$.
The QoI is the horizontal displacement $u_x$ of the top point of the half-cylinder, at $t = 1\,\mathrm{s}$ and at the steady state.

\paragraph{Reference.}
The case is redefined, so the published values do not apply.
A second implementation by co-authors (monolithic finite elements, FEniCSx; Supplementary Material, which also audits the small-strain definition) shares no code with solids4foam, but its displacement converges at only about first order, so it is a consistency check rather than a converged reference; hence, this reference is categorised as D (unquantified), and the evidence is self-convergence.

\paragraph{Numerical model.}
The fluid and solid meshes of the tutorial (480 and 120 cells) are the level L2 of a four-level study, L1--L4 (120 to 7\,680 fluid cells, both meshes refined together); the coupling is IQN-ILS with an interface predictor.

\paragraph{Results.}
At $t = 1\,\mathrm{s}$ on L2, $\Delta t = 0.25$ to $0.03125\,\mathrm{s}$ gives top-point $u_x = 0.0590302$, $0.0595007$, $0.0596084$ and $0.0596316\,\mathrm{m}$, with observed orders 2.13 and 2.21 ($u_x$) and 2.12 and 2.00 (interface position); below $\Delta t = 0.03125\,\mathrm{s}$ the changes ($\approx10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m}$) reach the coupling and solver tolerances.
L1--L4 give steady $u_x = 0.124852$, $0.135581$, $0.139075$ and $0.139948\,\mathrm{m}$, converging at an observed order of 2.00 to $0.14024\,\mathrm{m}$; L2 is $3.3\%$ and L4 $0.2\%$ below this value.
These values are collected in Table~\ref{tab:refvals_other} (Appendix~\ref{app:reference_values}).

\paragraph{Independent numerical comparisons.}
The FEniCSx implementation converges to limits within $0.05$--$0.19\%$ of ours for the top-point displacement, the interface forces and the pressure drop.

\paragraph{Lessons.}
A published definition with a small-strain solid at large deformation does not balance the interface forces, so the case is redefined here with a finite-strain solid.
No coupling study exists, and the temporal-order evidence partly overlaps with that of the ring added-mass problem and the collapsible channel.
