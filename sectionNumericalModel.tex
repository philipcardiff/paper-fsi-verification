All results in this paper were obtained with the partitioned fluid--solid interaction framework of the open-source toolbox solids4foam~\citep{Cardiff2025}, which builds on the OpenFOAM finite volume library~\citep{Weller1998} and follows the partitioned approach of \citet{Tukovic2018}.
The fluid and the solid are discretised on separate cell-centred finite volume meshes and solved by separate field solvers, which exchange interface displacements and tractions within each time step.
This section summarises the components that determine the discretisation, temporal and iterative errors studied in Sections~\ref{sec:verification_methodology}--\ref{sec:cross_benchmark}; implementation detail is in the source code and in the case set-ups that accompany each benchmark.
Case-specific choices (time scheme, convection scheme, solid variant, coupling algorithm and tolerances) are stated with each benchmark in Section~\ref{sec:benchmark_suite}.

%%--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------%%
\subsection{Fluid}\label{sec:fluid_solver}
%%--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------%%

The incompressible Navier--Stokes equations in ALE form (Section~\ref{sec:fluid_governing_eqn}) are solved by a segregated, pressure-based finite volume procedure (\texttt{pimpleFluid} in solids4foam, adapted from the OpenFOAM \texttt{pimpleFoam} solver).
Velocity and pressure are collocated at cell centres; the face fluxes are obtained by Rhie--Chow-type momentum interpolation~\citep{Rhie1983}, and the pressure--velocity coupling is the segregated PIMPLE algorithm (PISO pressure correctors within SIMPLE-type outer correctors), with the numbers of outer and pressure correctors stated per case.
Spatial operators are the standard second-order finite volume operators: midpoint quadrature, linear face interpolation, least-squares (most cases) or Gauss cell gradients, and Laplacian face fluxes with deferred non-orthogonal correction~\citep{Jasak1996}.
Convection uses central (\texttt{linear}) interpolation for the ring added-mass problem, the Womersley tube, the manufactured solution and the collapsible channel; linear-upwind interpolation with an unlimited least-squares gradient for FSI1, the Turek--Hron FSI3 sequence, the 3-D pressure-pulse tube, the flexible-bottom cavity and the blob in treacle; and, for the Hessenthaler flap, an unlimited blend of the two (\texttt{LUST}, Section~\ref{sec:hessenthaler}).
A gradient-limited linear-upwind scheme is used only for FSI2, the uniform FSI3 family of the coupling studies (Section~\ref{sec:fsi23}), the Mok lid-driven cavity results (its coupling-test study uses the unlimited form) and the beam in cross-flow.
In the flexible-bottom cavity the face flux of the continuity equation is also interpolated with skew correction (Section~\ref{sec:cavity}).
The time derivative of the transient problems is discretised with the second-order backward difference formula (BDF2, \texttt{backward}); first-order backward Euler (BDF1) is used only in the deliberate time-scheme comparison of Section~\ref{sec:3dtube}, and the steady problems are advanced in pseudo-time to the steady state.
The schemes of each case are those of its set-up at the commit listed in Appendix~\ref{app:provenance}.
On the moving mesh the convective flux is the relative flux $\bb{\Gamma}_f\cdot(\bb{v}_f - \bb{v}^\Gamma_f)$, where $\bb{\Gamma}_f$ is the face area vector and the mesh flux $\bb{\Gamma}_f\cdot\bb{v}^\Gamma_f$ is the volume swept by face $f$ per unit time, evaluated from the face swept volumes with the coefficients of the time scheme (BDF2 here).
With BDF2 the swept volumes of the last two steps are combined with the BDF2 weights, so that the discrete space-conservation law (Equation~\eqref{eqn:gcl}) holds for the backward volume derivative.
On the interface the fluid velocity equals the velocity of the moving wall, and the interface traction passed to the solid is the sum of the pressure and viscous tractions on the fluid interface faces.

On the OpenCFD OpenFOAM releases used here, the non-orthogonal correction of the wall-normal velocity gradient in the moving-wall and interface velocity conditions evaluates to zero, so on skewed near-wall cells the viscous wall traction carries an error of about $\nu\tan\theta\,|\partial\mathbf{u}/\partial\tau|$ ($\theta$ the non-orthogonality angle, $\tau$ the wall tangent), which does not vanish under refinement at fixed $\theta$ (Appendix~\ref{app:lessons}). Steady cases are unaffected, since at steady state the wall velocity is uniform along the wall. For the transient cases the effect was measured on Turek--Hron FSI3, whose flag combines a large tangential variation of the wall velocity with skewed near-wall cells: on a coarse block-structured mesh (11\,764 fluid cells), applying the correction changes the displacement means and amplitudes by at most $0.37\%$, the frequency by $0.06\%$ and the force means and amplitudes by at most $0.18\%$, well below the change on the first refinement of that mesh. The results reported here use the uncorrected conditions.

\subsection{Fluid mesh motion}\label{sec:fluid_mesh_motion}

The interior fluid mesh follows the interface through a Laplacian equation for the cell motion velocity (\texttt{velocityLaplacian}) or, where stated (the manufactured solution), for the cell displacement, whose diffusivity varies with the inverse (in most cases the inverse square) of the distance to the moving boundaries, so that cells near the interface move almost rigidly~\citep{Tukovic2018}.
For the Turek--Hron FSI3 sequence, on whose unstructured meshes these Laplacian equations fold cells at the full flag amplitude, the mesh is moved instead as a quasi-static linear elastic pseudo-solid (shear and bulk moduli in the ratio 1:3, with the stiffness scaled by the inverse distance to the cylinder and flag): the unknown is the total displacement of the points from the initial mesh, so the mesh for a given interface position does not depend on how the interface got there (Appendix~\ref{app:lessons}), and the nonlinear system, assembled on the current mesh, is solved by Newton--Krylov iterations (PETSc SNES).
The interface displacement is the solid displacement transferred to the fluid interface (Section~\ref{sec:interface_transfer}); the point motion is obtained from the cell motion velocity by interpolation, and the mesh points are moved once per coupling iteration.
Mesh motion is a numerical device: it changes the discrete solution only through the mesh quality and through the space-conservation and moving-wall treatments described above.

%%--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------%%
\subsection{Solid}\label{sec:solid_solver}
%%--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------%%

The solid momentum equation (Section~\ref{sec:solid_model}) is discretised by a cell-centred finite volume method with the total displacement as unknown~\citep{Jasak2000, Cardiff2021}.
Two kinematic formulations are used: a small-strain (linear geometry) formulation for the linear-elastic walls (the ring added-mass problem, the Womersley tube and the 3-D pressure-pulse tube), and a total Lagrangian formulation for the finite-strain problems, with St Venant--Kirchhoff (the Turek--Hron benchmarks, the collapsible channel and the Mok lid-driven cavity) or neo-Hookean (the Hessenthaler flap, the blob in treacle and the manufactured solution) material laws.
The time derivative uses BDF2, and each solid solve is a Newton--Krylov iteration through the PETSc SNES interface~\citep{PETSc, Cardiff2026}.
Two spatial variants are used.
The \emph{standard} variant uses a linear least-squares reconstruction of the displacement and a compact-stencil momentum stabilisation, a Rhie--Chow-type difference between the compact and the reconstructed face normal gradient, scaled by a user factor~\citep{Cardiff2026}; it is formally second order, but the stabilisation is not negligible on coarse meshes and stiffens thin, bending-dominated structures (Section~\ref{sec:fsi1}).
The \emph{high-order} variant reconstructs the displacement in each cell with a cubic (polynomial order 3) moving-least-squares fit on an extended stencil, and evaluates the face fluxes of the residual by quadrature of the reconstructed stress at points on each face~\citep{Batistic2026}.
This raises the order of the solid operator alone: the fluid discretisation, the BDF2 time integration and the interface load (Section~\ref{sec:interface_transfer}) are second order, so the coupled scheme remains formally second order.
Its benefit is the much smaller error constant for thin, bending-dominated structures: on the Turek--Hron flap under a static load the cubic variant is within $0.17\%$ of the converged tip deflection with 6 cells through the thickness, where the standard variant is $21.6\%$ too stiff (\texttt{paper-data/fsi1\_high\_order/flap}).
Which variant is used is stated per case, because for thin bending-dominated walls the choice can dominate the coupled error (Section~\ref{sec:collapsible}).

%%--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------%%
\subsection{Interface transfer}\label{sec:interface_transfer}
%%--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------%%

Displacements are transferred from the solid to the fluid interface at the interface points, and tractions from the fluid to the solid interface at the faces.
Where the two interface meshes coincide (the ring added-mass problem, the Womersley tube, the manufactured solution, the collapsible channel and the Mok lid-driven cavity), a direct one-to-one map is used.
Where they do not (the Turek--Hron benchmarks, the 3-D pressure-pulse tube and the Hessenthaler flap), face tractions are interpolated with arbitrary-mesh-interface (AMI) weights, and point displacements by barycentric interpolation on a triangulation of the donor interface faces.
The finite volume fluid delivers one area-averaged traction per face, and the solid receives one value per interface face.
With the high-order solid this value is applied uniformly at all quadrature points of the face, so the interface load is piecewise constant: it preserves the transferred force on every face and is second-order accurate, consistent with the fluid, but it does not carry the higher order of the solid operator to the coupled problem.
A higher-order load would require the fluid traction at the solid face quadrature points.
The interface transfer is part of the discretisation: it is an operator that must converge with refinement like any other, and a transfer whose weights depend on the absolute mesh scale can change with mesh level (Appendix~\ref{app:lessons}).

\subsection{Coupling algorithms}\label{sec:coupling_algorithms}
%%--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------%%

Strong coupling is enforced by outer (coupling) iterations within each time step.
Two interface formulations are used.

\paragraph{Dirichlet--Neumann.}
The fluid receives the interface displacement (mesh motion and wall velocity) and the solid receives the fluid traction.
Each time step starts with a solid predictor, i.e.\ a solid solve with the fluid traction of the previous step, whose displacement is passed to the fluid without relaxation in the first iteration~\citep{Tukovic2018}.
Subsequent interface displacements are updated by fixed under-relaxation, by Aitken dynamic relaxation~\citep{kuttler2008fixed}, or by the interface quasi-Newton inverse least-squares method (IQN-ILS)~\citep{Degroote2009, Degroote2013}, which builds an approximate inverse Jacobian of the interface residual from the secant information of the current and, optionally, previous time steps; secant columns that are repeated or nearly linearly dependent are filtered before the least-squares solve~\citep{Haelterman2016}.

\paragraph{Interface pressure condition.}
With Dirichlet--Neumann coupling the fluid interface is a moving wall on which the velocity is prescribed, so the pressure needs a Neumann condition.
On such a wall the relative convective flux vanishes, and the condition consistent with the momentum equation~\eqref{eqn:fluid_momentum_ale} is its wall-normal component,
\begin{equation}
	\hat{\bb{n}}_F\cdot\bb{\nabla} p_F = \hat{\bb{n}}_F\cdot\left(\bb{\nabla}\cdot\left\{\nu\left[\bb{\nabla}\bb{v}_F + (\bb{\nabla}\bb{v}_F)^T\right]\right\} + \bb{b}_F - \bb{a}^\Gamma\right),
	\label{eq:interface_pressure}
\end{equation}
where $\bb{a}^\Gamma$ is the acceleration of the wall.
The viscous term is evaluated explicitly, at the current outer iterate, in the cells next to the wall with the discrete operators of the momentum equation, and the body force with the source of the momentum equation.
Three forms of this condition appear in this paper: a zero normal gradient, which omits every term; the acceleration-only form, $\hat{\bb{n}}_F\cdot\bb{\nabla} p_F = -\hat{\bb{n}}_F\cdot\bb{a}^\Gamma$; and the full form~\eqref{eq:interface_pressure}.
Each omitted term leaves an $O(h)$ pressure error in the row of cells next to the interface (Appendix~\ref{app:lessons}).
With the full form, the face flux predicted on the interface uses the same prescribed gradient as the pressure equation, so the converged interface flux equals the mesh flux.
In an enclosed fluid domain the pressure equation has a solution only if the prescribed gradients are consistent with zero net flux through the boundary; the mean of the prescribed gradient over the boundary, weighted as in the pressure equation, is therefore removed. For an incompressible fluid the exact integral of $\hat{\bb{n}}_F\cdot\bb{a}^\Gamma$ over a closed interface is zero, so this removes only a discrete imbalance.

The interface pressure conditions are, case by case:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \emph{Full form~\eqref{eq:interface_pressure}}: the ring added-mass problem with both couplings (Section~\ref{sec:ring}, except the Robin--Neumann column of the tolerance sweep, Table~\ref{tab:tol_sweeps}), and the Robin--Neumann coupling of the manufactured solution (full variant, Table~\ref{tab:mms_fsi}).
	\item \emph{Acceleration only}: the Turek--Hron FSI3 Gmsh family, the IQN-ILS runs of the 3-D pressure-pulse tube, the primary solutions of the Hessenthaler flap, and every other Robin--Neumann run (the Womersley tube, the collapsible channel, FSI1, the uniform FSI3 family, the 3-D pressure-pulse tube and the flat variant of the manufactured solution).
	\item \emph{Zero normal gradient}: the remaining Dirichlet--Neumann runs (the Womersley tube, the collapsible channel, FSI1, FSI2, the uniform FSI3 family, the Mok lid-driven cavity, the flexible-bottom cavity, the beam in cross-flow and the blob in treacle) and the sensitivity check of the Hessenthaler flap. The Aitken runs of the manufactured solution use a flux-consistent gradient (Section~\ref{sec:mms_fsi}). In the steady cases the acceleration term vanishes, so the zero gradient differs from the full form only by the viscous and body-force terms.
\end{itemize}
The viscous term was not available when the acceleration-only results were computed.
Its effect, measured by recomputing with the full form, is small in the transient cases: on L1 of the FSI3 Gmsh family it changes the displacement and force means and amplitudes by at most $0.22\%$ (the $u_y$ amplitude by $-0.06\%$) and the frequency by $0.004\%$; it changes the 3-D pressure-pulse tube by $0.018\%$ of the peak displacement, and the collapsible channel by $0.1\%$ of the peak with IQN-ILS and $0.8\%$ with Robin--Neumann.
In the steady beam in cross-flow, where the viscous term is the only non-zero term, it changes the tip displacement by $+1.7\%$ on the mesh of the distributed case and by $+0.35\%$ on that mesh refined twice, so its effect falls with refinement, as an $O(h)$ boundary error should.
The Hessenthaler flap was not recomputed with the viscous term.

\paragraph{Robin--Neumann.}
The fluid pressure satisfies a Robin condition on the interface that accounts for the solid inertia through an estimate $\rho_S h_S$ of the solid interface impedance~\citep{BadiaNobile2008, Tukovic2018b}, and the iterations are unrelaxed fixed-point iterations.
The Robin condition contains the normal momentum balance~\eqref{eq:interface_pressure}, with the acceleration of the solid interface, and its viscous and body-force terms are included in the runs listed above.
The Robin condition modifies the discrete interface condition within the iterations; at convergence the interface flux is made consistent with the mesh motion.
The Robin condition changes the iteration rather than only accelerating it, so the converged Robin--Neumann and Dirichlet--Neumann solutions need not agree to the coupling tolerance (Section~\ref{sec:ref_hierarchy}).

\paragraph{Convergence test.}
The interface residual $\bb{r}$ is the difference between the solid interface point positions, transferred to the fluid interface, and the fluid interface point positions.
Two normalised measures are formed,
\begin{equation}
	r_1 = \frac{\|\bb{r}\|}{\max_k \|\bb{r}^{(k)}\|},
	\qquad
	r_2 = \frac{\|\bb{r}\|}{\|\bb{d}^\Gamma\|_{\max}},
	\label{eq:coupling_residuals}
\end{equation}
where the maximum in $r_1$ is over the coupling iterations $k$ of the current step and $\|\bb{d}^\Gamma\|_{\max}$ is the largest norm of the \emph{total} interface displacement seen so far.
Two acceptance tests are used, and they are named here by what they require.
The \emph{either-measure test}, the default of solids4foam, accepts a step when either measure is below the coupling tolerance, i.e.\ when $\min(r_1, r_2)$ is; the \emph{all-measure test} accepts it only when every measure is below the tolerance, i.e.\ when $\max(r_1, r_2)$ is.
The two tests differ greatly in slowly varying phases with a large total interface displacement, where $r_2$ can be small after a single relaxed iteration whatever the reduction of the residual within the step (Section~\ref{sec:iterative_errors}).
Robin--Neumann iterations additionally require the change of interface pressure and the interface leakage flux to fall below their own tolerances.
By default, a step that does not meet the test within the maximum number of iterations stops the run.

\subsection{Solution procedure and toolchain}\label{sec:implementation_details}
%%--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------%%

The partitioned procedure for one time step is summarised in Algorithm~\ref{alg:partitioned_fsi}.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Partitioned FSI procedure for one time step (solids4foam \texttt{fluidSolidInterface}).}
\label{alg:partitioned_fsi}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State Advance time; solve the solid with the fluid traction of the previous step (predictor).
\State Transfer the predicted interface displacement to the fluid interface.
\State For coupling iterations $k = 1, 2, \ldots$, up to the maximum number:
\State \hspace{1.5em}move the fluid mesh to the current interface displacement and update the mesh fluxes;
\State \hspace{1.5em}solve the fluid (PIMPLE outer and pressure correctors) with the moving-wall velocity, or with the Robin pressure condition;
\State \hspace{1.5em}transfer the fluid interface traction to the solid and solve the solid (Newton--Krylov);
\State \hspace{1.5em}transfer the solid interface displacement to the fluid interface and evaluate $\bb{r}$, $r_1$ and $r_2$;
\State \hspace{1.5em}stop if the convergence test (and, for Robin--Neumann, the pressure and flux tests) is met;
\State \hspace{1.5em}otherwise update the interface displacement: fixed relaxation, Aitken, IQN-ILS, or none (Robin--Neumann).
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The calculations used the OpenCFD releases OpenFOAM-v2412 and OpenFOAM-v2512 with PETSc, on Linux (x86\_64) and, for some of the inexpensive cases, macOS; the release, platform and solids4foam commit of each result are listed in Appendix~\ref{app:provenance}.
The compiler and its optimisation level are recorded as part of the configuration, since the default OpenFOAM build rules compile at \texttt{-O3}, whereas the EasyBuild toolchain used on one of the clusters compiles at \texttt{-O2}, and the same source can then produce executables that behave differently (Appendix~\ref{app:lessons}).
Table~\ref{tab:provenance} in Appendix~\ref{app:provenance} gives them per case.
